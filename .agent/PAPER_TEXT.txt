###### Abstract

Predicting cross-sectional stock returns is challenging due to low signal-to-noise ratios and evolving market regimes. Classical factor models offer interpretability but limited flexibility, while deep learning models achieve strong performance yet often underutilize financial priors. We address this gap with PRISM-VQ (PRior-Informed Stock Model with Vector Quantization), a dynamic factor framework that integrates expert prior factors, vector-quantized discrete latent factors learned from cross-sectional structure, and a structure-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts to generate time-varying factor loadings. Vector quantization acts as an information bottleneck that suppresses noise while capturing robust market structure, with discrete codes serving both as latent factors and as routing signals for temporal expert specialization. Experiments on CSI 300 and S&P 500 show consistent improvements in cross-sectional return prediction and portfolio performance over strong baselines while preserving interpretability. Our code is available at https://github.com/finxlab/PRISM-VQ.

## 1 Introduction

Predicting cross-sectional stock returns remains a fundamental challenge at the intersection of finance and machine learning due to persistently low signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), complex cross-asset dependencies, and frequent regime shifts Israel et al. (2020); Zhang and Hua (2025). Classical linear factor models Sharpe (1964); Fama and French (1992); Fama and French (1993) provide strong interpretability through economically grounded factors and loadings, but their limited expressiveness restricts their ability to capture nonlinear interactions and time-varying dynamics. This limitation has contributed to the proliferation of the so-called “factor zoo” Cochrane (2011), highlighting the difficulty of robust factor discovery.

Recent neural approaches to cross-sectional return prediction largely fall into two categories. Factor-based methods integrate neural networks within factor model structures. Autoencoder-based approaches Gu et al. (2021); Duan et al. (2022); Kim et al. (2025) learn latent factors via reconstruction objectives, but their reliance on continuous latent representations often provides insufficient regularization under low-SNR conditions and struggles with temporal non-stationarity. In contrast, architecture-centric deep learning methods employ modern sequence models such as Transformers Yoo et al. (2021); Li et al. (2024); Cao et al. (2024) to model spatio-temporal dependencies directly from data. Despite their expressive power, these models typically lack explicit structural priors, which reduces robustness under distribution shifts and limits interpretability.

Figure 1: Overview of the PRISM-VQ framework. Like a prism separating light, PRISM-VQ decomposes cross-sectional market structure using discrete vector quantization.

Across prior work, three recurring limitations emerge: (1) insufficient regularization of continuous latent representations in noisy environments; (2) structure-agnostic temporal modeling that fails to condition on evolving cross-sectional structure; and (3) underutilization of domain priors, where established financial knowledge is weakly exploited despite its stabilizing potential.

To address these limitations, we propose PRISM-VQ
(PRior-Informed Stock Model with Vector Quantization), a unified framework for cross-sectional stock return prediction that integrates discrete representation learning, structure-conditioned temporal modeling, and domain-informed priors within a factor-based interpretation (Figure 1). PRISM-VQ consists of three components. First, it learns vector-quantized latent factors through a joint vector quantization and contrastive learning objective Chen et al. (2020), where the learned codebook Van Den Oord et al. (2017) serves as an information bottleneck that suppresses noise while preserving cross-sectional structure. Second, it introduces a structure-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Shazeer et al. (2017) built on Transformer architectures, where discrete codes act as gating signals to generate dynamic factor loadings. Third, it incorporates domain-specific prior factors Jensen et al. (2023) as stabilizing anchors that maintain interpretability and improve robustness under distribution shifts.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

- •

We propose PRISM-VQ, a factor-based framework that combines financial prior factors, vector-quantized discrete latent factors, and structure-conditioned temporal modeling for cross-sectional stock rank prediction.

- •

We demonstrate that vector quantization provides an effective inductive bias for cross-sectional representation learning, consistently outperforming continuous latent alternatives in noisy financial settings.

- •

We introduce a discrete-code-gated MoE mechanism that enables structure-conditioned dynamic factor loading generation while preserving a clear factor interpretation and supporting scalable conditional computation.

- •

We conduct experiments on CSI 300 and S&P 500, where PRISM-VQ achieves the state-of-the-art performance, including RankIC of 0.0646 and 0.0141, and Sharpe ratios of 1.57 and 0.67, respectively.

## 2 Related Works

### 2.1 Machine Learning-Based Factor Models

Factor models have long been central to asset pricing, from the CAPM Sharpe (1964) to the Fama–French models Fama and French (1992).
Although interpretable, their linear structure limits expressiveness in modern markets. Autoencoder asset pricing Gu et al. (2021) introduced neural networks to learn nonlinear factor structures, followed by extensions such as FactorVAE Duan et al. (2022) and FactorVQVAE Kim et al. (2025), which employs vector quantization Van Den Oord et al. (2017) for discrete latent factors.
Later work incorporated additional structure, including graph-based industry modeling Duan et al. (2025) and regime-aware dynamics Xiang et al. (2024).
Despite these advances, most methods remain autoencoder-based, limiting temporal modeling capacity and underutilizing financial priors. PRISM-VQ addresses these limitations by combining discrete factor learning with Transformer-based temporal modeling and explicit domain-informed priors.

### 2.2 Deep Learning for Stock Prediction

Another line of work adopts end-to-end deep learning without explicit factor structures.
Transformer-based models have been particularly influential.
DTML Yoo et al. (2021) modeled inter-stock dependencies with attention, MASTER Li et al. (2024) alternated intra- and inter-stock attention, and MATCC Cao et al. (2024) incorporated trend decomposition.
While effective, these models typically lack interpretable factor representations, weakly encode domain knowledge, and show limited robustness under regime shifts.
More recently, explicit relational modeling has been explored through temporal-relational graphs and hypergraph structures Chen et al. (2024); Song et al. (2025); Alaygut and Sefer (2025).
These methods rely on predefined or extracted relational topologies, whereas PRISM-VQ learns implicit cross-sectional structure through a discrete codebook without requiring relational preprocessing, enabling transferability across markets.
PRISM-VQ retains the expressive power of Transformers while preserving factor interpretability by using them to generate dynamic factor loadings conditioned on learned cross-sectional structure.

### 2.3 Representation Learning for Finance

The low SNRs of financial data place strong demands on representation learning.
Contrastive learning has been explored to learn robust stock embeddings Du et al. (2024); Hwang et al. (2023).
Vector quantization offers a complementary approach by introducing a discrete information bottleneck that suppresses noise and promotes stable pattern reuse.
Recent work has applied vector quantization to identify persistent market structure; for example, STORM Zhao et al. (2024) employs dual VQ-VAEs to capture spatio-temporal patterns, while FactorVQVAE Kim et al. (2025) introduces discrete latent factors via a single-stage VQ objective. PRISM-VQ differs from both by combining contrastive-regularized VQ with structure-conditioned MoE routing and explicit financial priors within a unified two-stage framework.
To our knowledge, PRISM-VQ is the first framework to integrate vector-quantized discrete factors, contrastive representation learning, expert prior factors, and discrete-code-conditioned temporal modeling within a unified factor-based modeling framework.

## 3 Problem Formulation

We study cross-sectional stock return prediction. At each decision time tt, the objective is to predict returns for NtN_{t} stocks over the next Δ\Delta days for ranking-based portfolio construction. Unless otherwise specified, all quantities are measured at time tt, and we omit the explicit time index in symbols such as xix_{i}, XX, fpf_{p}, yiy_{i}, and y^i\hat{y}_{i}.

### 3.1 Data and Factor Model Framework

Each stock ii is associated with temporal features xi∈ℝT×Cx_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}, where TT is the lookback window and CC is the feature dimension.
Stacking all stocks yields the cross-sectional tensor
X={xi}i=1Nt∈ℝNt×T×CX=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times T\times C}.
We also observe PP expert prior factors fp∈ℝPf_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}, such as value, size, and momentum. The prediction target yiy_{i} corresponds to the return from t+1t+1 to t+Δt+\Delta.

Returns are modeled using a dynamic factor structure:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | yi=αi+βp,iT​fp+βl,iT​fl,i+ϵi,y_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{p,i}^{T}f_{p}+\beta_{l,i}^{T}f_{l,i}+\epsilon_{i}, | | (1) |

where αi\alpha_{i} is the intercept, βp,i\beta_{p,i} and βl,i\beta_{l,i} are loadings on prior and learned factors, fl,if_{l,i} denotes learned latent factors, and ϵi\epsilon_{i} is idiosyncratic noise. Unlike classical factor models with static linear loadings, both loadings and latent factors are generated by nonlinear mappings:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | (αi,βp,i,βl,i)\displaystyle(\alpha_{i},\beta_{p,i},\beta_{l,i}) | =Φloading​(xi,fp,θ),\displaystyle=\Phi_{\text{loading}}(x_{i},f_{p};\theta), | | (2) |
| | fl,i\displaystyle f_{l,i} | =Φfactor​(xi,fp,θ),\displaystyle=\Phi_{\text{factor}}(x_{i},f_{p};\theta), | | (3) |

where Φloading\Phi_{\text{loading}} and Φfactor\Phi_{\text{factor}} are deep neural networks with parameters θ\theta.

### 3.2 Modeling Challenges

Cross-sectional stock return prediction faces several challenges: (i) extremely low SNRs, (ii) complex and evolving cross-sectional dependencies, (iii) non-stationary market regimes requiring adaptive factor loadings, and (iv) the need for interpretability consistent with financial theory. PRISM-VQ addresses these challenges through a two-stage learning paradigm, where the spatial learning stage discovers discrete latent factors via vector quantization and the temporal learning stage generates dynamic factor loadings using structure-conditioned expert networks.

## 4 Proposed Model

### 4.1 Overview: Two-Stage Learning

PRISM-VQ decouples cross-sectional structure discovery from time-varying factor loading generation through a two-stage framework. Stage 1 (Spatial Learning) learns a discrete codebook and stock-level assignments via vector quantization, mapping codes to latent factor values. Stage 2 (Temporal Learning) generates dynamic factor loadings using a Transformer-based MoE, with expert routing conditioned on the discrete codes. After Stage 1, the codebook is fixed, while stock assignments may vary over time (Figure 2).

Figure 2: Architecture of PRISM-VQ. The spatial learning stage (left) learns discrete stock representations via vector quantization over cross-sectional features. The temporal learning stage (right) uses the resulting discrete codes to gate expert networks and generate dynamic factor loadings that combine expert prior factors with learned latent factors for return prediction.

### 4.2 Spatial Learning: Discrete Cross-Sectional Factor Discovery

#### 4.2.1 Cross-Sectional Feature Extraction

Each stock ii is associated with features xi∈ℝT×Cx_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}. We first encode temporal dynamics using a GRU encoder ϕgru\phi_{\text{gru}}:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | hi=ϕgru​(xi)∈ℝds.\displaystyle h_{i}=\phi_{\text{gru}}(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}. | | (7) |

To capture cross-sectional interactions at time tt, we stack all embeddings into
H=[h1;…;hNt]∈ℝNt×dsH=[h_{1};\ldots;h_{N_{t}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times d_{s}} and apply a cross-asset Transformer encoder ℰ\mathcal{E}:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | z=ℰ⁡(H)∈ℝNt×ds,zi∈ℝds.\displaystyle z=\mathcal{E}(H)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times d_{s}},\quad z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}. | | (8) |

Through self-attention, each ziz_{i} aggregates information from related stocks, forming a representation suitable for discrete clustering.

#### 4.2.2 Vector Quantization for Discrete Factor Learning

We discretize the continuous embeddings using a learnable codebook
𝒵={𝒄k}k=1K⊂ℝds\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{c}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}.
Each embedding is assigned to its nearest codeword:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ki=argmink∈{1,…,K}∥zi−𝒄k∥22,zq,i=𝒄ki.\displaystyle k_{i}=\argmin_{k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\bigl\lVert z_{i}-\bm{c}_{k}\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2},\quad z_{q,i}=\bm{c}_{k_{i}}. | | (9) |

This discretization introduces a finite prototype space that acts as a strong inductive bias under low SNRs. It (i) suppresses idiosyncratic noise by snapping embeddings to recurring cross-sectional prototypes, (ii) limits effective model capacity through a bounded set of codewords, and (iii) encourages sample sharing among stocks assigned to the identical code, yielding stable and interpretable clusters. Since codewords are reused over time, the learned codes naturally separate persistent cross-sectional structures from time-varying factor sensitivities in the two-stage design.

To enable end-to-end training despite the non-differentiable assignment, we adopt the straight-through estimator Van Den Oord et al. (2017) and optimize

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ℒVQ=‖sg​(z)−zq‖22+λcommit​‖z−sg​(zq)‖22,\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{VQ}}=\\|\text{sg}(z)-z_{q}\\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\text{commit}}\\|z-\text{sg}(z_{q})\\|_{2}^{2}, | | (10) |

where sg​(⋅)\text{sg}(\cdot) denotes the stop-gradient operator.

#### 4.2.3 Contrastive Learning for Semantic Clustering

Nearest-neighbor quantization alone may yield arbitrary partitions. We therefore introduce a codebook contrastive objective Chen et al. (2020):

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ℒcontra=−log⁡exp⁡(sim​(zi,𝒄ki)/τ)∑k=1Kexp⁡(sim​(zi,𝒄k)/τ),\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{contra}}=-\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_{i},\bm{c}_{k_{i}})/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\text{sim}(z_{i},\bm{c}_{k})/\tau)}, | | (11) |

where τ\tau is a temperature parameter and sim​(u,v)=−‖u−v‖2\text{sim}(u,v)=-\|u-v\|_{2}. This objective contrasts each embedding against all codeword prototypes, explicitly enlarging the margin between the assigned codeword and competing codewords. As a result, clustering is guided by cross-sectional semantics rather than purely geometric proximity.

#### 4.2.4 Reconstruction and Auxiliary Prediction

Pure clustering can produce stable but weakly predictive representations, while pure prediction may fail to extract reusable structure. We therefore introduce two auxiliary tasks to regularize the discrete codes, zq,iz_{q,i}.

##### Reconstruction.

A decoder 𝒢\mathcal{G} reconstructs the input features from the quantized code, conditioned on prior factors using FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation) Perez et al. (2018):

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | x^i\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i} | =𝒢⁡(zq,i,fp),\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}(z_{q,i},f_{p}), | | |
| | ℒrecon\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} | =1Nt​∑i=1Nt‖xi−x^i‖22.\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\\|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\\|_{2}^{2}. | | (12) |

This conditioning encourages the learned codes to remain consistent with the expert prior information.

##### Multi-horizon prediction.

To retain forward-looking signals, we further regularize the codes through multi-horizon return prediction:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | [\displaystyle[ | y^i,1,…,y^i,Nh]=ψpred(zq,i,fp),\displaystyle\hat{y}_{i,1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{i,N_{h}}]=\psi_{\text{pred}}(z_{q,i},f_{p}), | | |
| | | ℒpred=1Nt​Nh​∑i=1Nt∑h=1Nh(yi,h−y^i,h)2.\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}=\frac{1}{N_{t}N_{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\sum_{h=1}^{N_{h}}(y_{i,h}-\hat{y}_{i,h})^{2}. | | (13) |

Then, the following multi-task objective is optimized:

Stage 1 outputs the discrete codes zq,iz_{q,i} and the corresponding latent factor values fl,if_{l,i} via Eq. (5), which are subsequently used in Stage 2.

### 4.4 Two-Stage Training Procedure

##### Stage 1: Spatial learning.

We optimize ℒspatial\mathcal{L}_{\text{spatial}} in Eq. (14) to learn the temporal encoder ϕgru\phi_{\text{gru}}, cross-asset encoder ℰ\mathcal{E}, codebook 𝒵\mathcal{Z}, and auxiliary modules 𝒢\mathcal{G} and ψpred\psi_{\text{pred}}.
This stage produces discrete codes zq,iz_{q,i} and latent factor values fl,if_{l,i}.

##### Stage 2: Temporal learning.

We fix the learned codebook 𝒵\mathcal{Z} and optimize ℒtemporal\mathcal{L}_{\text{temporal}} in Eq. (23) to train the temporal Transformer 𝒯\mathcal{T}, MoE gating and experts, and the loading projection ψload\psi_{\text{load}}.
Discrete codes from Stage 1 act as structural conditioning signals for expert routing and factor construction.

##### Design rationale.

The two stages are decoupled to avoid codebook collapse Van Den Oord et al. (2017) and to enforce an information bottleneck. Stage 1 compresses cross-sectional variation into a stable discrete structure, while Stage 2 conditions temporal modeling on this representation without co-adapting the codebook.11

## 5 Experiments and Results

We evaluate PRISM-VQ on two major stock markets and conduct a comprehensive empirical study to assess its effectiveness, robustness, and interpretability.
All reported results are computed on a strictly out-of-sample test period spanning 2022 to 2024, using identical data preprocessing pipelines and train-validation-test splits across all methods.

##### Research Questions.

Our experiments are designed to address the following questions:

- •

RQ1 (Overall effectiveness). Does PRISM-VQ improve cross-sectional rank prediction and portfolio performances compared to strong baseline methods?

- •

RQ2 (Component validity). How do the three core components, namely expert prior factors, the discrete codebook, and the structure-conditioned MoE, individually contribute to overall performance?

- •

RQ3 (Robustness). How sensitive is PRISM-VQ to key hyperparameters, including codebook size, number of experts, and portfolio top-KK selection?

- •

RQ4 (Interpretability). Do the learned discrete codes capture economically meaningful structure beyond standard factor taxonomies?

- •

RQ5 (Expert specialization). Do different experts specialize in distinct market conditions while maintaining stable behavior over time?

### 5.1 Experimental Setting

#### 5.1.1 Datasets and Protocol

We evaluate PRISM-VQ on two major stock markets, CSI 300 (China) and S&P 500 (U.S.), using data sourced from Qlib Yang et al. (2020). At each trading date tt, we use contemporaneous index constituents to avoid survivorship bias. Input features are Alpha158 factors with a lookback window of T=20T=20 days.
The prediction target is the 5-day forward return, defined as yi,t=(pricei,t+5−pricei,t+1)/pricei,t+1y_{i,t}=(\text{price}_{i,t+5}-\text{price}_{i,t+1})/\text{price}_{i,t+1}, using the open price at t+1t+1 and the close price at t+5t+5. We additionally include 1- to 9-day forward returns as auxiliary targets for ℒpred\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}. Expert prior factors consist of P=13P{=}13 factor returns from the JKP Global Factor Library Jensen et al. (2023), computed using information available up to t−1t-1 and aggregated as 20-day cumulative values.22
2

Details on factor construction and preprocessing are provided in Technical Appendix B.

We adopt chronological splits with training from 2009 to 2019, validation from 2020 to 2021, and testing from 2022 to 2024. Features are normalized using RobustZScoreNorm, missing values are forward-filled with Fillna, and training targets are cross-sectionally rank-normalized per date using CSRankNorm.

#### 5.1.2 Baselines

We compare PRISM-VQ against representative baselines from three categories.
General machine learning methods include XGBoost Chen and Guestrin (2016), GRU Chung et al. (2014), TCN Lea et al. (2017), and Transformer Vaswani et al. (2017).
Factor-based methods include CAE Gu et al. (2021), VAE Duan et al. (2022), and VQVAE Kim et al. (2025).
Architecture-centric deep learning methods include DTML Yoo et al. (2021), MASTER Li et al. (2024), and MATCC Cao et al. (2024). All baselines use identical data splits and preprocessing pipelines, with hyperparameters tuned on the validation set.

#### 5.1.3 Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate performance using two complementary criteria. First, cross-sectional ranking accuracy is measured by daily RankIC and RankICIR. Second, portfolio performance is evaluated using a TopKK-DropNN strategy with K=30K{=}30 and N=5N{=}5, reporting annualized return (AR), maximum drawdown (MDD), and Sharpe ratio.33
3

Formal definitions of ranking and portfolio evaluation metrics are given in Technical Appendix C, while details of the portfolio construction protocol follow Technical Appendix D. All portfolio results incorporate transaction costs of 5 basis points at open and 15 basis points at close.

#### 5.1.4 Implementation Details

PRISM-VQ is implemented in PyTorch Paszke et al. (2019) and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. All experiments are repeated with five random seeds. In Stage 1, we use a representation dimension ds=128d_{s}{=}128 with a GRU encoder, a VQ codebook of size K=512K{=}512, and a cross-asset Transformer with one layer and two attention heads. This stage is trained for up to 50 epochs. In Stage 2, we use a temporal dimension dt=64d_{t}{=}64 and a temporal Transformer with RoPE, using two heads for CSI 300 and four heads for S&P 500, with dropout set to 0.1. The Transformer is followed by an MoE of lightweight MLP experts, with Me=2M_{e}{=}2 (top-1) for CSI 300 and Me=8M_{e}{=}8 (top-4) for S&P 500. Stage 2 is trained for up to 50 epochs with the codebook fixed. Optimization is performed using AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter (2017) with a learning rate 10−410^{-4}, LambdaLR scheduling, gradient clipping at 1.0, and early stopping with patience 15. We set λcommit=0.25\lambda_{\text{commit}}{=}0.25, λcontra=1\lambda_{\text{contra}}{=}1, λpred=10−4\lambda_{\text{pred}}{=}10^{-4}, and λbalance=10−2\lambda_{\text{balance}}{=}10^{-2} for CSI 300 and 10−310^{-3} for S&P 500.

## 6 Conclusion

We proposed PRISM-VQ, a two-stage framework for cross-sectional stock rank prediction that learns reusable discrete cross-sectional structure via vector quantization with contrastive regularization and generates time-varying factor loadings through structure-conditioned MoE routing guided by financial priors. Across CSI 300 and S&P 500, PRISM-VQ consistently improves prediction and portfolio performance over strong baselines while producing interpretable discrete codes and specialized expert behaviors. These results indicate that combining a discrete information bottleneck with prior-informed conditional computation is an effective design principle for low-SNR, non-stationary financial return prediction problems.

## Appendix A Architecture and Implementation Details

We first describe the neural building blocks shared across model components and then explain how they are instantiated within each learning stage.

Throughout this appendix, we follow the notation of the main text. Stage 1 (spatial learning) employs a GRU encoder ϕgru\phi_{\text{gru}}, a cross-asset Transformer encoder ℰ\mathcal{E}, and a vector-quantization (VQ) codebook 𝒵\mathcal{Z} to produce discrete stock representations zq,i∈ℝdsz_{q,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}. Stage 2 (temporal learning) uses a temporal Transformer 𝒯\mathcal{T} together with a structure-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) to generate dynamic factor loadings 𝜷i=(αi,βp,i,βl,i)\bm{\beta}_{i}=(\alpha_{i},\beta_{p,i},\beta_{l,i}).

Unless stated otherwise, experiments use a lookback window of T=20T{=}20 trading days, C=158C{=}158 Alpha158 input features, P=13P{=}13 expert prior factors, a spatial latent dimension of ds=128d_{s}{=}128, a temporal hidden dimension of dt=64d_{t}{=}64, and a VQ codebook of size K=512K{=}512.

### A.1 Shared Model Building Blocks

This subsection describes the neural components reused across both stages of PRISM-VQ. These include Transformer encoder blocks for modeling cross-sectional and temporal dependencies, as well as the positional encoding mechanism used for sequential inputs.

#### A.1.1 Transformer Encoder Block

Let X∈ℝN×dX\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} denote a matrix of NN input tokens with a hidden dimension of dd. In Stage 1, tokens correspond to stocks in the cross section at a fixed time. In Stage 2, tokens correspond to temporal observations for a single stock. Each Transformer encoder block consists of a multi-head self-attention (MHA) layer followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN), with residual connections and layer normalization applied after each sub-layer.
This architecture enables flexible modeling of interactions while maintaining stable optimization.

We use nhn_{h} attention heads, each with a per-head dimension of dh=d/nhd_{h}=d/n_{h}.

##### Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHA).

For each attention head h∈{1,…,nh}h\in\{1,\ldots,n_{h}\}, queries, keys, and values are computed via linear projections:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | Q(h)=X​WQ(h),K(h)=X​WK(h),V(h)=X​WV(h),\displaystyle Q^{(h)}=XW_{Q}^{(h)},\quad K^{(h)}=XW_{K}^{(h)},\quad V^{(h)}=XW_{V}^{(h)}, | | |
| | WQ(h),WK(h),WV(h)∈ℝd×dh,Q(h),K(h),V(h)∈ℝN×dh.\displaystyle W_{Q}^{(h)},W_{K}^{(h)},W_{V}^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{h}},\quad Q^{(h)},K^{(h)},V^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{h}}. | | (A.1) |

Scaled dot-product attention computes pairwise interactions between tokens:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | A(h)\displaystyle A^{(h)} | =softmax⁡(Q(h)​K(h)⊤dh)∈ℝN×N,\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q^{(h)}{K^{(h)}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}, | | |
| | head(h)\displaystyle\mathrm{head}^{(h)} | =A(h)​V(h)∈ℝN×dh.\displaystyle=A^{(h)}V^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{h}}. | | (A.2) |

The scaling by dh\sqrt{d_{h}} prevents attention scores from growing excessively large and stabilizes gradients.

Outputs from all heads are concatenated and projected back to dimension dd:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | MHA⁡(X)\displaystyle\mathrm{MHA}(X) | =Concat⁡(head(1),…,head(nh))​WO,\displaystyle=\mathrm{Concat}\big(\mathrm{head}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathrm{head}^{(n_{h})}\big)\,W_{O}, | | |
| | | WO∈ℝd×d.\displaystyle\qquad W_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}. | | (A.3) |

##### Feed-Forward Network (FFN).

The FFN applies a position-wise nonlinear transformation independently to each token:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | FFN⁡(x)\displaystyle\mathrm{FFN}(x) | =W2​GELU​(W1​x+b1)+b2,\displaystyle=W_{2}\,\mathrm{GELU}(W_{1}x+b_{1})+b_{2}, | | |
| | | W1∈ℝd×dff,W2∈ℝdff×d,\displaystyle\qquad W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\mathrm{ff}}},\;W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{ff}}\times d}, | | (A.4) |

where dffd_{\mathrm{ff}} denotes the intermediate expansion dimension, typically chosen as a multiple of dd.
The GELU nonlinearity provides smooth activation and empirically improves optimization stability in Transformers.

##### Encoder block structure.

Each Transformer encoder block combines the above components with residual connections and layer normalization:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | X~\displaystyle\tilde{X} | =LayerNorm⁡(X+MHA⁡(X)),\displaystyle=\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\big(X+\mathrm{MHA}(X)\big), | | |
| | X′\displaystyle X^{\prime} | =LayerNorm⁡(X~+FFN⁡(X~)).\displaystyle=\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\big(\tilde{X}+\mathrm{FFN}(\tilde{X})\big). | | (A.5) |

Residual connections preserve information flow across layers, while layer normalization mitigates covariate shift and improves training stability.

#### A.1.2 Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE)

To encode temporal order in sequential inputs, PRISM-VQ adopts Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) Su et al. [2024].
Unlike absolute positional encodings, RoPE injects relative positional information directly into the attention mechanism, allowing attention scores to depend on the relative distances between time steps.

RoPE is applied independently to each attention head and operates on the per-head dimension dhd_{h}.
For a query or key vector u∈ℝdhu\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}} at position index mm, RoPE applies a sequence of two-dimensional rotations:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | RoPE⁡(u,m)=BlockDiag⁡(Rm,1,…,Rm,dh/2)​u,\displaystyle\mathrm{RoPE}(u,m)=\mathrm{BlockDiag}(R_{m,1},\ldots,R_{m,d_{h}/2})\,u, | | (A.6) |

where each rotation matrix is defined as

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | Rm,j\displaystyle R_{m,j} | =(cos⁡(m​θj)−sin⁡(m​θj)sin⁡(m​θj)cos⁡(m​θj)),\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(m\theta_{j})&-\sin(m\theta_{j})\\ \sin(m\theta_{j})&\cos(m\theta_{j})\end{pmatrix}, | | |
| | θj\displaystyle\theta_{j} | =10000−2(j−1)/dh,j∈{1,…,dh/2}.\displaystyle=10000^{-2(j-1)/d_{h}},\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,d_{h}/2\}. | | (A.7) |

In practice, the RoPE transformation replaces the original Q(h)Q^{(h)} and K(h)K^{(h)} vectors in Eq. (A.2) before attention computation.
This enables the model to generalize to unseen sequence lengths and improves robustness when modeling non-stationary financial time series.

### A.3 Stage 1 (Spatial Learning): Quantizer, Decoder, and Auxiliary Prediction

This section describes the implementation details of Stage 1, which learns discrete cross-sectional representations and associated latent factor values.

##### Input normalization (RevIN).

Before encoding, each stock’s input sequence xi∈ℝT×Cx_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C} is normalized using reversible instance normalization (RevIN) Kim et al. [2021].
RevIN normalizes each time series independently using per-sample statistics and applies the corresponding inverse transform to the reconstructed output.
This mitigates non-stationarity across stocks and time without leaking future information.

##### Cross-asset encoding.

At each date tt, the GRU encoder ϕgru\phi_{\text{gru}} maps temporal features xi∈ℝT×Cx_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C} to a fixed-length embedding hi∈ℝdsh_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}} (Eq. (7)). Stacking all stocks yields the cross-sectional matrix H∈ℝNt×dsH\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times d_{s}}. This matrix is processed by the cross-asset Transformer ℰ\mathcal{E}, implemented as a stack of encoder blocks (Eq. (A.5)), to produce context-aware embeddings zi∈ℝdsz_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}} (Eq. (8)).
Self-attention enables each stock representation to incorporate information from related stocks in the same cross section.

##### Vector quantization.

Each continuous embedding zi∈ℝdsz_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}} is discretized by assigning it to the nearest codeword 𝒄k\bm{c}_{k} in the codebook 𝒵={𝒄k}k=1K\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{c}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ki\displaystyle k_{i} | =arg⁡mink∈{1,…,K}⁡‖zi−𝒄k‖22,zq,i=𝒄ki∈ℝds.\displaystyle=\arg\min_{k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\\|z_{i}-\bm{c}_{k}\\|_{2}^{2},\quad z_{q,i}=\bm{c}_{k_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}. | | (A.8) |

Training follows Eq. (10) with a commitment weight λcommit=0.25\lambda_{\text{commit}}{=}0.25 and the straight-through estimator.
To encourage balanced code usage, an exponential moving average (EMA) of code assignments is maintained, and underutilized codes are periodically re-initialized via probability-based anchoring. The codebook contrastive loss (Eq. (11)) is applied with a temperature τ=0.07\tau=0.07, explicitly separating code prototypes in representation space.

##### FiLM-conditioned reconstruction decoder 𝒢\mathcal{G}.

The reconstruction decoder enforces that discrete codes preserve information necessary to recover the original input. Given a quantized code zq,iz_{q,i} and prior factors fpf_{p}, the decoder reconstructs x^i=𝒢⁡(zq,i,fp)\hat{x}_{i}=\mathcal{G}(z_{q,i},f_{p}) (Eq. (12)) using a FiLM-conditioned multi-resolution 1D upsampling architecture.

First, zq,i∈ℝdsz_{q,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}} is projected into a low-resolution hidden sequence:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | Xi(0)=reshape⁡(GELU⁡(Win​zq,i)),whereX(0)i∈ℝH×T0,Win∈ℝ(H​T0)×ds,\begin{split}X^{(0)}_{i}&=\mathrm{reshape}\big(\mathrm{GELU}(W_{\mathrm{in}}z_{q,i})\big),\\ \text{where}\quad&X^{(0)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times T_{0}},\quad W_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(HT_{0})\times d_{s}},\end{split} | | (A.9) |

where H=128H{=}128 and T0=5T_{0}{=}5. We then apply Ku=log2⁡(T/T0)K_{u}=\log_{2}(T/T_{0}) upsampling blocks (with T=20T{=}20, so Ku=2K_{u}{=}2). Each block performs channel expansion, PixelShuffle upsampling, FiLM conditioning on fpf_{p}, and a transposed-convolution skip connection. FiLM generates per-channel affine parameters (γ,β)∈ℝH(\gamma,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{H} and applies
X←X⊙(1+γ)+βX\leftarrow X\odot(1+\gamma)+\beta
in residual form.
A final 1×11{\times}1 convolution projects the output to C=158C{=}158 channels, yielding x^i∈ℝT×C\hat{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}.

##### Multi-horizon auxiliary prediction ψpred\psi_{\text{pred}}.

To encourage predictive structure in the learned codes, Stage 1 includes an auxiliary multi-horizon return prediction task.
The predictor ψpred​(zq,i,fp)\psi_{\text{pred}}(z_{q,i},f_{p}) (Eq. (13)) is implemented as a GRU-based autoregressive decoder.
An MLP maps the concatenated vector [zq,i;fp][z_{q,i};f_{p}] to the initial GRU hidden state.
A learnable start token is used to autoregressively generate NhN_{h} future returns.
This auxiliary objective is weighted by λpred=10−4\lambda_{\text{pred}}{=}10^{-4} and serves as a weak supervision signal that aligns discrete codes with future return dynamics.

### A.4 Stage 2 (Temporal Learning): Temporal Encoding and Structure-Conditioned MoE

Stage 2 focuses on modeling time-varying dynamics and generating dynamic factor loadings conditioned on both temporal information and learned cross-sectional structure.
Unlike Stage 1, which is frozen after training, all parameters in Stage 2 are optimized end-to-end.

##### Temporal encoder 𝒯\mathcal{T}.

Stage 2 consumes the same input window xi∈ℝT×Cx_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C} as Stage 1, together with the discrete code zq,i∈ℝdsz_{q,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}} produced by the fixed codebook.
Following Eq. (16), cross-sectional structure is injected into temporal modeling by prepending a structure token derived from zq,iz_{q,i}.
When ds≠dtd_{s}\neq d_{t}, the code is first projected to ℝdt\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}} via a linear layer.

The resulting token sequence is processed by a Transformer encoder 𝒯\mathcal{T} equipped with Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE; Appendix A.1.2), which allows attention scores to depend on relative temporal positions.
The temporal stream is first linearly mapped to ℝdt\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}} and then passed through 𝒯\mathcal{T} (1 encoder layer; FFN dimension 128; dropout 0.1).
We use 2 attention heads for CSI 300 and 4 heads for S&P 500.
The output corresponding to the structure token is taken as a compact temporal summary
htemp,i∈ℝdth_{\mathrm{temp},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}, which aggregates both temporal dynamics and cross-sectional context.

##### Structure-conditioned MoE and loading head ψload\psi_{\text{load}}.

Dynamic factor loadings are generated using a structure-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE).
The MoE decouples expert selection, which depends on discrete structural codes, from expert computation, which depends on temporal information.
This design allows different experts to specialize in distinct market regimes associated with different codes.

##### Fusion to form expert inputs.

The temporal summary htemp,ih_{\mathrm{temp},i} and discrete code zq,iz_{q,i} are first normalized:
h~i=LN⁡(htemp,i)∈ℝdt\tilde{h}_{i}=\mathrm{LN}(h_{\mathrm{temp},i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}} and
z~i=LN⁡(zq,i)∈ℝds\tilde{z}_{i}=\mathrm{LN}(z_{q,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}.
These are concatenated and projected through a small residual MLP to form the expert input:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ui=ϕproj​([h~i;z~i])∈ℝdt.\displaystyle u_{i}=\phi_{\mathrm{proj}}([\tilde{h}_{i};\tilde{z}_{i}])\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}. | | (A.10) |

The projection module ϕproj\phi_{\mathrm{proj}} consists of a residual feed-forward block with GEGLU-style gating and dropout (0.1), which improves expressiveness while maintaining training stability.

##### Code-conditioned sparse routing.

Expert routing depends solely on the discrete code, enforcing a clear separation between structural conditioning and temporal computation.
The gate computes logits from z~i\tilde{z}_{i} and selects the top-kk experts (stochastic via Eqs. (17)–(18) at training, deterministic ℓi=μi\ell_{i}=\mu_{i} at inference):

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ℓi\displaystyle\ell_{i} | =ϕgate​(z~i)∈ℝMe,𝒦i=Topk​(ℓi),\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{gate}}(\tilde{z}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{e}},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{i}=\mathrm{Top}_{k}(\ell_{i}), | | |
| | Gi,j\displaystyle G_{i,j} | ={exp⁡(ℓi,j)∑ℓ∈𝒦iexp⁡(ℓi,ℓ)if ​j∈𝒦i,0otherwise.\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\exp(\ell_{i,j})}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{K}_{i}}\exp(\ell_{i,\ell})}&\text{if }j\in\mathcal{K}_{i},\\[6.0pt] 0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases} | | (A.11) |

This yields a sparse probability vector GiG_{i} with ‖Gi‖0=k\|G_{i}\|_{0}=k and ∑jGi,j=1\sum_{j}G_{i,j}=1.
Each expert ξj\xi_{j} is a lightweight MLP mapping ui∈ℝdtu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}} to ℝdmoe\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{moe}}}, with hidden size dmoe=64d_{\mathrm{moe}}{=}64 and dropout 0.1.
Expert outputs are aggregated as

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | mi=∑j=1MeGi,j​ξj​(ui)∈ℝdmoe.\displaystyle m_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{e}}G_{i,j}\,\xi_{j}(u_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{moe}}}. | | (A.12) |

We use Me=2M_{e}{=}2 experts with top-1 routing for CSI 300, and Me=8M_{e}{=}8 experts with top-4 routing for S&P 500, reflecting differences in market complexity and signal heterogeneity.

##### Dynamic loadings via base–modulation decomposition.

Rather than predicting (αi,βp,i,βl,i)(\alpha_{i},\beta_{p,i},\beta_{l,i}) directly, the loading head decomposes generation into a temporal base and a code-conditioned modulation.
Base loadings are derived from temporal information:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | βp,ibase=Wpbase​htemp,i∈ℝP,βl,ibase=Wlbase​htemp,i∈ℝds.\displaystyle\beta^{\mathrm{base}}_{p,i}=W^{\mathrm{base}}_{p}h_{\mathrm{temp},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},\quad\beta^{\mathrm{base}}_{l,i}=W^{\mathrm{base}}_{l}h_{\mathrm{temp},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}. | | (A.13) |

The MoE output mim_{i} predicts FiLM-style modulation parameters:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | (γp,i,δp,i)\displaystyle(\gamma_{p,i},\delta_{p,i}) | =ϕp​(mi)∈ℝ2​P,\displaystyle=\phi_{p}(m_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2P}, | | |
| | (γl,i,δl,i)\displaystyle(\gamma_{l,i},\delta_{l,i}) | =ϕl​(mi)∈ℝ2​ds.\displaystyle=\phi_{l}(m_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d_{s}}. | | (A.14) |

Final dynamic loadings are produced by residual affine modulation:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | βp,i=γp,i⊙βp,ibase+δp,i,βl,i=γl,i⊙βl,ibase+δl,i.\displaystyle\beta_{p,i}=\gamma_{p,i}\odot\beta^{\mathrm{base}}_{p,i}+\delta_{p,i},\quad\beta_{l,i}=\gamma_{l,i}\odot\beta^{\mathrm{base}}_{l,i}+\delta_{l,i}. | | (A.15) |

The intercept is predicted as αi=wα⊤​mi\alpha_{i}=w_{\alpha}^{\top}m_{i}.
This decomposition improves stability and interpretability by separating time-varying effects from structure-dependent adjustments.

##### Regularization and training objective.

Stage 2 is trained using the temporal objective in Eq. (23).
In addition to the standard MoE load-balancing auxiliary loss, an ℓ2\ell_{2} penalty is applied to the loadings,
‖βp,i‖2+‖βl,i‖2\|\beta_{p,i}\|_{2}+\|\beta_{l,i}\|_{2}, to discourage excessive exposure magnitudes.
Final return prediction follows Eq. (22).

## Appendix B Expert Prior Factors

PRISM-VQ incorporates a set of expert-designed financial factors as domain-informed priors. Specifically, we use 13 factor return series from the JKP Global Factor Library77
7

https://jkpfactors.com/, a widely used and publicly documented collection of global equity factors Jensen et al. [2023]. The selected factors span multiple dimensions of firm characteristics and return anomalies:

- •

accruals: Measures the extent to which earnings are driven by accounting accruals rather than cash flows; high accruals are often associated with lower future returns.

- •

debt_issuance: Captures changes in corporate leverage through net debt issuance; firms issuing more debt tend to underperform subsequently.

- •

investment: Reflects asset growth or capital investment intensity; high investment rates are empirically linked to lower expected returns.

- •

low_leverage: Favors firms with lower financial leverage, which are typically less exposed to distress risk.

- •

low_risk: Proxies for low-volatility or low-beta characteristics; low-risk stocks have been shown to earn anomalously high risk-adjusted returns.

- •

momentum: Measures past intermediate-horizon returns; stocks with strong recent performance tend to continue outperforming in the short to medium term.

- •

profit_growth: Captures growth in operating profitability; firms with improving profitability often experience positive revaluation.

- •

profitability: Measures the level of operating profits relative to assets or equity; highly profitable firms tend to earn higher average returns.

- •

quality: Aggregates multiple firm characteristics such as profitability, earnings stability, and conservative investment behavior.

- •

seasonality: Exploits recurring calendar-based return patterns, such as month-of-the-year effects.

- •

short_term_reversal: Captures mean-reversion in very recent returns; stocks that underperform in the short term often rebound.

- •

size: Represents firm size, typically measured by market capitalization; smaller firms historically earn higher average returns.

- •

value: Measures valuation ratios such as book-to-market; cheaper stocks tend to outperform growth stocks over long horizons.

Each factor is treated as a market-wide factor return series, rather than as a stock-specific characteristic. These factors serve as economically interpretable anchors that encode well-established return drivers from asset pricing theory.
Throughout construction, we strictly enforce a no–look-ahead constraint: all factor values used at decision time tt are computed exclusively from information available up to t−1t-1.

##### Rolling cumulative return construction (excluding current day).

Let rj,τr_{j,\tau} denote the daily return of factor jj on trading date τ\tau.
To align factor inputs with the model lookback window T=20T{=}20 and ensure temporal consistency with stock-level features, we construct prior factor inputs as rolling cumulative returns over the previous TT trading days, excluding the current day.

Concretely, factor jj at decision time tt is defined as

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | fp,j​(t)\displaystyle f_{p,j}(t) | =exp⁡(∑τ=t−Tt−1log⁡(1+rj,τ))−1.\displaystyle=\exp\!\left(\sum_{\tau=t-T}^{t-1}\log\!\left(1+r_{j,\tau}\right)\right)-1. | | (B.1) |

This formulation is equivalent to the multiplicative return
∏τ=t−Tt−1(1+rj,τ)−1\prod_{\tau=t-T}^{t-1}(1+r_{j,\tau})-1,
implemented in log space for numerical stability.
By shifting the window to end at t−1t-1, the construction explicitly excludes contemporaneous factor information, preventing any form of look-ahead bias.

##### Normalization and usage.

All factor series are standardized using statistics computed on the training period only. The resulting vector fp​(t)∈ℝPf_{p}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{P} is provided as an input to both Stage 1 and Stage 2. In Stage 1, prior factors condition reconstruction and auxiliary prediction, encouraging discrete codes to remain compatible with known economic structure. In Stage 2, they enter the factor pricing equation directly via βp,i⊤​fp​(t)\beta_{p,i}^{\top}f_{p}(t), preserving a transparent link between expert priors and predicted returns.

This design allows PRISM-VQ to leverage established financial knowledge for stability and interpretability while still learning complementary structure-driven signals through vector-quantized latent factors.

## Appendix C Evaluation Metrics

This appendix details the evaluation metrics used in Section 5.
Model performance is assessed from two complementary perspectives:
(i) cross-sectional ranking quality and
(ii) portfolio-level utility under a fixed trading protocol.
All expectations and standard deviations are computed over the test period.
The number of tradable stocks NtN_{t} may vary over time due to index reconstitution.

### C.1 Ranking Performance Metrics

We evaluate cross-sectional ranking accuracy using RankIC and its information ratio, RankICIR.
The model output y^i,t\hat{y}_{i,t} is interpreted as a ranking score rather than a calibrated return forecast.
Accordingly, metrics focus on the relative ordering of stocks within each date.

##### RankIC.

RankIC is defined as the Spearman rank correlation between predicted scores and realized returns in the cross section.
At each date tt, we compute RankIC by applying the Pearson correlation to within-date ranks:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | RankICt\displaystyle\text{RankIC}_{t} | =(ry^t−r¯y^t)⊤​(ryt−r¯yt)Nt⋅σy^t⋅σyt,\displaystyle=\frac{(r_{\hat{y}_{t}}-\bar{r}_{\hat{y}_{t}})^{\top}(r_{y_{t}}-\bar{r}_{y_{t}})}{N_{t}\cdot\sigma_{\hat{y}_{t}}\cdot\sigma_{y_{t}}}, | | (C.1) |

where ry^t,ryt∈ℝNtr_{\hat{y}_{t}},r_{y_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}} denote the within-date ranks of predicted scores and realized returns, respectively (rank 1 corresponds to the highest value).
Here, r¯\bar{r} and σ\sigma denote the within-date mean and standard deviation of ranks.
This formulation ensures that RankIC is invariant to monotonic transformations of y^i,t\hat{y}_{i,t} and is robust to scale differences across dates.

The reported RankIC is the time average over the test period:

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | RankIC | =1Ttest​∑t=1TtestRankICt.\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{test}}}\text{RankIC}_{t}. | | (C.2) |

##### RankICIR.

To measure the stability and consistency of ranking performance over time, we report RankICIR, defined as

| | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | RankICIR | =𝔼⁡[RankICt]std⁡(RankICt).\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{RankIC}_{t}]}{\mathrm{std}(\text{RankIC}_{t})}. | | (C.3) |

RankICIR penalizes models with volatile daily ranking performance, even if their average RankIC is high.
This metric is particularly relevant in financial applications, where consistent signal quality is crucial for portfolio construction and risk management.

Overall, RankIC captures the average cross-sectional predictive power, while RankICIR reflects its temporal reliability.

### C.2 Portfolio Performance Metrics

Portfolio performance is evaluated using the same TopKK–DropNN rebalancing protocol described in Section 5.
At each date tt, let 𝒫t\mathcal{P}_{t} denote the set of held stocks after ranking and filtering, and let wi,tw_{i,t} be the portfolio weight assigned to stock i∈𝒫ti\in\mathcal{P}_{t}.
Unless otherwise specified, portfolios are equal-weighted, i.e., wi,t=1/|𝒫t|w_{i,t}=1/|\mathcal{P}_{t}|.

##### Daily portfolio return.

Given realized stock returns yi,ty_{i,t}, the portfolio log return is computed as

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | gp,t=log⁡(1+∑i∈𝒫twi,t​yi,t),g_{p,t}=\log\!\left(1+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}w_{i,t}\,y_{i,t}\right), | | (C.4) |

which ensures time additivity and numerical stability when aggregating returns across periods.

##### Annualized return (AR).

Annualized return is computed from the mean daily log return assuming 252 trading days per year:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | AR=exp⁡(252⋅𝔼⁡[gp,t])−1.\text{AR}=\exp\!\big(252\cdot\mathbb{E}[g_{p,t}]\big)-1. | | (C.5) |

##### Maximum drawdown (MDD).

Let Wt=exp⁡(∑τ=1tgp,τ)W_{t}=\exp\!\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}g_{p,\tau}\right) denote the cumulative wealth process. Maximum drawdown measures the largest peak-to-trough loss over the evaluation horizon:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | MDD=maxt⁡(1−Wtmaxs≤t⁡Ws).\text{MDD}=\max_{t}\!\left(1-\frac{W_{t}}{\max_{s\leq t}W_{s}}\right). | | (C.6) |

MDD captures downside risk and is particularly relevant for evaluating robustness under adverse market conditions.

##### Sharpe ratio (SR).

Risk-adjusted performance is measured using the Sharpe ratio:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | SR=252⋅𝔼⁡[gp,t]std⁡(gp,t),\text{SR}=\frac{\sqrt{252}\cdot\mathbb{E}[g_{p,t}]}{\mathrm{std}(g_{p,t})}, | | (C.7) |

where the denominator is the standard deviation of daily portfolio log returns. The risk-free rate is set to zero, following common practice in comparative ranking-based studies.

##### Transaction costs.

Transaction costs are applied at each rebalance according to the protocol specified in Section 5, and are fully incorporated into gp,tg_{p,t} before computing all reported portfolio metrics.

## Appendix D Portfolio Construction

Portfolio performance is evaluated using Qlib’s TopkDropoutStrategy88
8

https://qlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/component/strategy.html, also referred to as the TopKK–DropNN protocol.
This strategy converts cross-sectional ranking scores into a realistic, turnover-controlled trading portfolio.

##### Ranking-based candidate selection.

Let 𝒰t\mathcal{U}_{t} denote the tradable universe at trading day tt, and let y^i,t\hat{y}_{i,t} be the predicted ranking score for stock ii.
We first identify the top-ranked candidate set

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | 𝒞t=TopK⁡({y^i,t}i∈𝒰t,Kport),\mathcal{C}_{t}=\operatorname{TopK}\!\left(\{\hat{y}_{i,t}\}_{i\in\mathcal{U}_{t}},\,K_{\text{port}}\right), | | (D.1) |

which contains the KportK_{\text{port}} stocks with the highest predicted scores on day tt.

##### Turnover-constrained rebalancing.

Let 𝒫t−1\mathcal{P}_{t-1} denote the portfolio holdings from the previous trading day.
The TopKK–DropNN strategy updates the portfolio in two steps:
(i) it sells up to NdropN_{\text{drop}} currently held stocks with the lowest scores, and (ii) it replaces them with the same number of higher-ranked stocks from 𝒞t\mathcal{C}_{t} that are not already held.
This procedure enforces an explicit turnover constraint by limiting the number of daily replacements to at most NdropN_{\text{drop}}.

##### Portfolio weights.

After rebalancing, the portfolio size is restored to KportK_{\text{port}}, and equal weights are assigned:
wi,t=1/|𝒫t|w_{i,t}=1/|\mathcal{P}_{t}| for all i∈𝒫ti\in\mathcal{P}_{t}.
Unless otherwise stated, all experiments use equal-weighted portfolios.

## Appendix H Code Transition Dynamics

Figure H.1: Code transition probability matrices over monthly (left) and quarterly (right) horizons for the top 100 most active codes in CSI 300 (top) and S&P 500 (bottom). Diagonal entries indicate persistence, while entropy summarizes transition dispersion.

To assess the temporal stability of the learned discrete representations, code transition probabilities are analyzed over monthly and quarterly horizons.
Figure H.1 reports empirical transition matrices for the top 100 most frequently assigned codes in each market.
Diagonal dominance reflects persistence: at the monthly horizon, the average diagonal mass is 4.9% for CSI 300 and 7.4% for S&P 500, substantially exceeding the uniform baseline of 1%.
Persistence further increases at the quarterly horizon (11.7% for CSI 300 and 13.8% for S&P 500), indicating that the learned codes capture stable cross-sectional structure rather than transient noise.

The two markets exhibit distinct transition characteristics.
CSI 300 shows lower persistence but more concentrated transitions, with lower transition entropy (2.27), suggesting that stocks switch codes more frequently but tend to move among a limited set of successor states.
In contrast, S&P 500 exhibits higher persistence together with more dispersed transitions (entropy 2.99), indicating that stocks remain in the same code for longer periods but explore a broader set of alternative states when transitions occur.
These differences are consistent with the market-specific structure-conditioned MoE configurations described in the main text, where the more efficient U.S. market benefits from greater expert diversity.
