Financial time-series forecasting is important for portfolio construction, risk management, and investment strategy design, but
financial markets are highly nonstationary and often lack stable
periodicity. Retrieval-augmented forecasting is attractive in this
setting because similar market conditions can lead to similar historical market responses. However, when retrieval is performed
over the raw signal, similarity can be dominated by level or trend
rather than by detrended temporal patterns that are more relevant
to future market reactions. We propose RISE (Retrieve the Right
Signal: Decomposition-Guided Retrieval-Augmented Forecasting
for Financial Markets), a decomposition-guided retrieval framework for financial time-series forecasting. RISE decomposes each
input into a trend component and a detrended component using
a strictly causal moving average, and performs retrieval in the detrended space. The retrieved detrended futures are used both as
a direct retrieval-based forecasting component and as Retrievalas-Context (RAC) tokens that provide additional context to the
forecasting backbone. This design separates trend modeling from
retrieval over detrended temporal patterns, reducing misleading
raw-signal matches while preserving historical market responses

Introduction

Recent advances in deep learning have substantially refined timeseries forecasting. Traditional statistical models typically rely on
explicit assumptions or structures to model autocorrelation, trend,

volatility, and other temporal dependencies. Modern deep-learning
models learn complex temporal dependencies through architectures such as MLPs, CNNs, Transformers, decomposition-based
models, and patch-based models. Three directions stand out in
recent work. The first is decomposition-based forecasting (e.g.,
DLinear [19], Autoformer [18]), which separates a series into trend
and seasonal or residual components and models the resulting components separately. The second is patch-based forecasting (e.g.,
PatchTST [12]), which slices the series into short segments and
efficiently learns temporal interactions among nearby time steps.
The third is retrieval-augmented forecasting (e.g., RAFT [7]), which
retrieves past segments resembling the present and uses what followed them to inform the prediction.
Yet these approaches have largely been developed and validated
on standard benchmarks with relatively stable periodicity, such as
electricity, weather, and traffic. Financial series, by contrast, behave
differently.
In financial forecasting [15, 16], predictive information is often
less tied to the trend component or stable periodicity than to detrended temporal patterns around the trend [4, 6, 10]. Downstream
tasks, including portfolio construction, volatility estimation [1, 5],
risk management [4, 15, 16], and investment strategy design [15, 16],
rely more heavily on detrended temporal patterns that reflect shortterm market responses than on long-term price levels. Although
such detrended temporal patterns rarely form stable periodic cycles, comparable historical conditions can lead to similar historical
market responses.
This recurrence makes retrieval-based forecasting attractive for
financial time series. By directly referring to past windows with
similar detrended temporal patterns, retrieval mechanisms can use
historical market responses more explicitly than models that store
temporal structure only implicitly in their parameters. However,
retrieval over the raw signal has a structural limitation in finance.
Because financial trends vary substantially across macroeconomic
regimes, raw-signal similarity can be dominated by level or trend
rather than by detrended temporal patterns. Consequently, the
retrieved windows may appear similar in overall level or trend
while the retrieved detrended futures fail to align with the historical
market response relevant to the current query. Figure 1 shows this
failure mode on the S&P 500: raw-signal retrieval finds a highercorrelation neighbor whose future direction contradicts the ground
truth. Detrended retrieval finds a lower-correlation neighbor whose
future direction is aligned with the query.
To address these issues, we propose RISE: Retrieve the Right
Signal, a decomposition-guided retrieval framework for financial
time-series forecasting. The key idea is that retrieval should focus
on detrended temporal patterns rather than on raw-signal similarity dominated by level or trend. RISE therefore decomposes the
input into a trend component and a detrended component, and
confines retrieval to the detrended space. This focuses retrieval on
the detrended component, reducing misleading matches caused by
raw-signal similarity dominated by level or trend while preserving
detrended temporal patterns.
RISE further performs multi-granularity retrieval to capture detrended temporal patterns at different temporal resolutions. The
retrieved detrended futures are aggregated by similarity and used

Related Work

Financial time-series forecasting has long been studied through
both traditional statistical models and deep-learning approaches.
ARIMA models capture autocorrelation and trend-based patterns,
ARCH/GARCH models describe conditional volatility [1, 5], and
VAR models capture dependencies among macroeconomic variables [15]. Random-walk and random-walk-with-drift baselines

have also served as simple but strong references, reflecting the
efficient-market view that market prices already incorporate available information [6, 10] and illustrating the empirical difficulty of
predicting financial markets [11]. Deep-learning models such as
RNNs and LSTMs have been used to learn temporal dependencies in
financial time series, while recent approaches such as MASTER [8],
MATCC [3], and DoubleAdapt [20] have advanced cross-sectional
stock return prediction and ranking-oriented settings.
Recent deep time-series forecasting models have explored several architectural directions. Decomposition-based methods such
as DLinear [19] and Autoformer [18] separate a series into trend
and seasonal or residual components and model the resulting
components separately. These methods make component separation explicit, but they mainly use the separated components
for forecasting rather than for deciding which component should
serve as the basis for historical comparison. Patch-based models
such as PatchTST [12] divide the input into short segments to
learn temporal interactions among nearby time steps. Transformerbased and multiscale architectures, including iTransformer [9]
and TimeMixer [17], further improve forecasting by redesigning
attention or mixing mechanisms for long-range temporal modeling. DUET [14] is also related as a clustering-based multivariate
forecasting method that exploits structured grouping in time-series
data. These architectures improve representation or structural
modeling, but they do not explicitly retrieve similar historical
market responses in financial time series.
Retrieval-augmented forecasting provides another direction by
connecting the current segment with similar past segments and
using what followed them to inform the prediction. RAFT [7] is
a representative method in this line, retrieving past windows resembling the present and incorporating the corresponding future
information into the forecast. GTR [2] is also related in that it
exploits historical patterns or memory, although it is closer to using learned global cycle representations than to explicit nearestneighbor retrieval. These methods introduce historical information
into forecasting, but they do not explicitly address which signal
component should be used for retrieval in financial time series,
where raw-signal similarity can be dominated by level or trend.
Unlike these prior approaches, RISE combines decomposition
and retrieval by using the detrended component as the retrieval
target in financial time-series forecasting. This design distinguishes
RISE from decomposition-based models that use separated components mainly for forecasting, patch-based models that encode temporal patterns implicitly, and retrieval-based models that typically
retrieve from the raw signal or raw-signal-derived representations.

Evaluation protocol and data leakage mitigation. To ensure a fair comparison across all evaluated models, the two-stage
normalization detailed in Section 3 is applied identically: global
per-channel z-score statistics are computed strictly on the training
partition, and the terminal input value is subtracted locally during
sample construction.
For RISE specifically, three additional measures strictly preclude
look-ahead bias. First, the offline memory bank is populated exclusively with sliding windows from the training split, ensuring that
validation and test queries can only retrieve historically observed
memory bank entries. Second, during training, memory entries
whose input-future intervals overlap with the query window are
excluded from retrieval, preventing any query from accessing temporally adjacent training windows (Section 3.4). Third, detrended
retrieved futures are precomputed offline employing the identical
deterministic causal moving average (Eq. (8)) utilized during online
inference, ensuring that neither the decomposition process nor the
retrieval candidates are exposed to validation or test data.
Implementation details. All deep-learning experiments are
conducted on two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−4 , a batch size of 32, and a CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts scheduler over 10 epochs. We evaluate predictive horizons 𝐻 ∈ {8, 16, 24, 36, 48, 60}, corresponding approximately to 1.5 weeks to 3 months.
To reduce evaluation asymmetries from disparate hyperparameter tuning budgets, Table 2 evaluates all deep-learning models
under a controlled protocol with a fixed look-back length (𝐿=96)
and reports averages over five random seeds {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}. For RISE
and RAFT, top-𝑚 is selected via grid search over {1, 5, 10, 20} and
fixed to 20 in the main results; for RAFT, we use three retrieval
periods {1, 2, 4}.
For RISE, the default configuration uses 𝑘=25, patch length 𝑝=16,
stride 𝑠=8, embedding dimension 𝑑=128, a 2-layer Transformer
encoder with 4 attention heads, 𝐺=3 granularities with pooling
widths {4, 2, 1}, and softmax temperature 𝜏=0.1.

4.4

Ablation Studies

We conduct two targeted ablation studies: (i) a component ablation isolating the contribution of each predictive pathway, and
(ii) a diagnostic comparison of retrieval similarity keys to assess
whether retrieval should be performed in the raw price space or
the detrended local-dynamics space.
(i) Component ablation. We evaluate four ablated variants
of RISE on five representative real-world datasets: w/o Patch, w/o
Trend, w/o Retrieval, and w/o Trend & Retrieval (Table 4). All variants
are retrained under the standard hyperparameter configuration.
Excluding the patch predictor incurs the most severe performance degradation (+5.2% overall increase in MSE), indicating that
the patch pathway contributes most to overall predictive performance. Ablating the linear trend predictor or the retrieval pathway
individually incurs a moderate penalty of 0.6–1.3%. Removing both
simultaneously yields a larger combined penalty (+2.1%) than removing either pathway alone, suggesting that the two pathways
provide partially non-overlapping predictive signals within the full
model.

retrieval-only comparison between two variants: (a) Raw retrieval,
which computes similarity over the raw input window, and (b) Detrended retrieval, which computes similarity over the causal-MA
detrended signal. Both variants use the same nearest-neighbor selection and softmax-weighted aggregation rule. This analysis does

Empirical evidence. Across eight real-world financial datasets,
RISE attains the highest first-place count in both MSE and MAE
(23/48 each), nearly triple the next best neural baseline, and pairwise outperforms all deep-learning baselines on 71–100% of settings.
On the independent TFB financial-market benchmark (NASDAQ,
NYSE), RISE attains the lowest overall MSE (.657) and ranks first
in 7/8 MSE configurations. A retrieval-key diagnostic (Table 5) further isolates the contribution of decomposition: while raw and
detrended retrieval are essentially tied on aggregate MSE (.130 vs.
.131), detrended retrieval improves directional accuracy on seven of
eight datasets (.560 vs. .537), directly substantiating the motivating
example (Figure 1). Component ablations confirm that the patch,
trend, and retrieval pathways provide partially non-overlapping
signals; RISE is robust to retrieval hyperparameters (≤ 1.0% variation), while a shorter look-back (𝐿=36) further reduces MSE by
3.9% over our default 𝐿=96, indicating that the reported margins
under protocol-parity are conservative.
Limitations. RISE’s predictive advantage diminishes in regimes
where historical similarity offers limited actionable signal: a naive
drift baseline dominates EUR/USD (consistent with the Meese–
Rogoff puzzle), and RAFT’s raw-signal retrieval outperforms RISE
on Hang Seng and on Bitcoin’s volatility-burst regime (by 10.1%
in average MSE on the latter), yielding a slightly lower Overall Avg MSE for RAFT (.1198 vs. .1221). Our dataset suite also
under-represents mainland-Chinese equities, energy commodities,
and broader FX pairs. These limitations motivate learnable assetadaptive decomposition filters and robust retrieval protocols for
near-random-walk and high-volatility regimes, as well as extending
RISE to intraday and microstructural forecasting.
More broadly, RISE suggests that decomposition in retrievalaugmented forecasting is best understood as a division of labor:
retrieval over the detrended residual specializes in directional structure, while a separate trend pathway handles absolute level. The
empirical effectiveness of this design is not visible in any single
retrieval metric alone, but emerges once directional accuracy and
integration with a learned representation are considered jointly.

Conclusion

We introduced RISE, a decomposition-guided retrieval architecture
for financial time-series forecasting. RISE uses a strictly causal
trend–detrended decomposition as a routing mechanism: the macro
trend is directed to a channel-independent linear head, while the
detrended residual is processed in parallel by a multi-granularity
similarity-based retrieval module and a patch-based Transformer,
with the three pathway outputs combined via a learnable weighted
fusion. The retrieved detrended futures are also injected as Retrievalas-Context (RAC) tokens into the patch encoder, allowing retrieval
