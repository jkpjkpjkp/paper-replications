Abstract

1

Graph-derived domain signals create a placement problem in learned
decision systems: they may inform the predictor, or they may enter
the downstream decision layer directly. We study this question in
learned portfolio contracts, where a graph-risk score can be supplied as a neural feature (Feature-only contract) or additionally
priced as an explicit penalty on risky exposure (Graph-penalty
contract). To separate placement from asset-specific graph-score
ordering, we introduce a within-state permutation diagnostic. The
control permutes the graph-risk scores across assets within each decision state, preserving the state-level marginal distribution and the
explicit penalty placement while breaking the asset-specific graphrisk assignment. On Fama-French industry portfolios and an external universe of exchange-traded funds (ETFs), explicit penalty placement reduces conditional value-at-risk at the 95% level (CVaR95),
crowding, and co-crash risk relative to feature-only placement over
ten seeds, with essentially matched effort. All variants use matched
architecture, decision rule, benchmark, and feasibility constraints.
The diagnostic separates two channels: explicit penalty placement,
not graph ordering, accounts for the aggregate CVaR reduction;
the permutation control attains lower CVaR than the true ordering
in both universes, while the true ordering helps most clearly for
industry crowding. We therefore identify explicit decision-layer
placement as the more reliable source of risk control in this setting.

Graph-derived signals are increasingly used in learned decision systems, but standard feature-based use does not determine whether
the signal should also enter the downstream decision rule directly.
We study this question in delegated portfolio risk control, an instance of graph-based decision support where a graph-risk score
can remain in the feature map or be additionally priced through
an explicit exposure penalty in the decision layer. Financial graph
signals model relations among traded assets [18, 25]; in risk-control
settings, these relations often encode tail dependence, crowded
trading, and systemic exposure in financial networks [1, 12, 19]. In
portfolio-delegation contracts, a principal compensates an agent
for output while limiting downside and systemic exposure [5, 16].
The key question is placement; the randomized-score comparison
diagnoses whether the graph-derived ordering adds metric-specific
value beyond that placement.
Much of financial graph learning concentrates on the signal itself, through richer graph estimators, attention mechanisms, or
tail-aware encoders. The implicit assumption is that more informative signals propagate to better decisions. A flexible neural learner
can in principle use such a signal through its feature map, but its
marginal effect is then learned indirectly and can be entangled with
other predictors, finite-sample noise, and the training objective. The
same signal written into the contract changes the marginal price of
risky exposure by construction. Standard feature-removal ablations
cannot separate these two channels: they conflate the loss from absent information with the loss from absent placement, leaving open
whether placement effects exist beyond signal informativeness.
Related work on decision-focused learning trains predictive components through downstream decisions [9, 20, 22, 24], while differentiable optimization layers embed optimization problems inside
neural architectures [3, 6]. Algorithmic contract design instead
studies how to compute globally optimal contracts under specified
information structures [10, 14, 23]. Our goal is different: we fix the
contract’s functional form, learn its coefficients, and study where
in the contract a graph-risk signal should enter. The contribution
is therefore a placement–ordering diagnostic.
We instantiate the diagnostic in portfolio delegation, treating
industry portfolios or exchange-traded funds (ETFs) as delegated
assets. A learner maps asset features and a pre-contract graph-risk
score to payment-rule coefficients; the agent’s best-response exposure is computed by an analytic feasibility step that enforces
incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and a minimum effort bound. The graph-penalty contract is first compared with the
feature-only contract to measure explicit penalty placement, and

Our contributions are as follows:
• Separable placement and ordering channels. We frame
the value of a graph-derived signal in a learned decision rule
as two separately testable design questions: where the signal
enters the decision rule, and how it ranks assets within a
decision state. This framing exposes distinct failure modes:
a graph score can be informative yet weakly placed, or explicitly placed but metric-specific in its ordering value.
• Within-state permutation control. We introduce a scoremarginal-matched randomized control that preserves the
within-state score distribution and explicit penalty placement while breaking asset-specific ordering. Unlike featureremoval ablations, this control tests graph-specific value
under the same explicit penalty placement.
• Two-universe empirical evidence. We instantiate the diagnostic in Fama–French industry portfolios and an external ETF universe, showing that explicit penalty placement
consistently reduces downside and systemic-risk metrics,
while graph-specific value is metric-dependent. Effort checks
and node-level exposure diagnostics support the intended
exposure-reweighting channel.

Empirical Placement–Ordering Diagnostic

Data and evaluation protocol. We evaluate on two traded-asset
universes. The main universe is the Fama-French 49-industry portfolio panel [13]; a universe of 20 ETFs, constructed from daily
adjusted ETF prices from Yahoo Finance, provides an external validation setting [26]. The ETF ticker list and preprocessing rules are
provided in the artifact. Each decision state uses a 252-day lookback
and a 20-day horizon. Training uses 50 bootstrap scenario paths
per decision state for computational efficiency. Reported test-set
risk metrics are computed by re-evaluating the trained models with
500 common bootstrap scenario paths per state. Residual shocks
𝜖 ∈ R𝑁 are propagated as 𝜖˜ = (𝐼 𝑁 − 𝜌𝑊𝑡 ) −1𝜖, where 𝑊𝑡 is the
row-normalized version of 𝐴𝑡 and 𝜌 = 0.25 is the propagation parameter. The industry split is chronological: train 1990–2005 (190
states), validation 2006–2007 (25 states), and test 2008–2026Q1 (228
states). The ETF split uses train 2015–2018 (28 states), validation
2019 (13 states), and test 2020–2026Q1 (77 states). Learned models
are trained over ten fixed seeds.
The learner is a two-hidden-layer ReLU network of width 32,
trained with Adam [21], with 𝑧𝑖 ∈ [0.80, 1.20] enforcing the effortpreserving range. All learned variants solve the feasibility system

Conclusion

Whether a domain signal is informative and where it should enter
a learned decision rule are separately testable design questions,
and the within-state permutation diagnostic separates them. In
portfolio-delegation contracts on Fama–French industries and ETFs,
placement yields directionally consistent risk reductions across all
six universe–metric cells, with the same sign across every seed.
The explicit penalty channel, isolated by a constant-score control,
accounts for the aggregate CVaR reduction, while the graph-score
multiplier carries the crowding benefit, clearest in the industry
universe. We view these as distinct design dimensions: progress
on graph estimators and progress on signal placement should be
evaluated separately, and a learner that already consumes a graph
score may still benefit from explicit placement. A natural next step
is to characterize when cross-sectional graph ordering survives
the downstream best-response map and adds value beyond explicit
placement, and to use that characterization to co-design dynamic
or learned graph estimators with the decision layer.
