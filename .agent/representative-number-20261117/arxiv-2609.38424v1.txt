# Graph Anomaly Detection as Finite-Horizon Control:

Training-Free Scoring via Empirical Bayes

###### Abstract

Node-level graph anomaly detection (GAD) identifies nodes whose attributes and interactions deviate from dominant graph regularities. Existing GAD models encode normality and anomaly scoring indirectly through architectures, message passing, reconstruction or contrastive objectives, and tuned score families. This entangles graph trust (how strongly graph structure should define normality), graph-spectral weighting, and anomaly-score choice, yielding scores that are costly, opaque, and unstable across graph regimes. We propose EB-GAD (Empirical-Bayes GAD), a training-free framework that models normality as graph-aware generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (GOU) relaxation toward a graph-filtered template. Empirical Bayes fits the graph precision from the residual-field likelihood; the GOU then turns scoring into a closed-form finite-horizon control energy, the minimum effort to steer a feature-neutral node to its observed endpoint along graph-spectral relaxation. Sweeping relaxation horizon and endpoint tolerance yields a bank of scores that share one fitted prior: equilibrium Mahalanobis scoring is one limit, while finite-horizon control-energy and scale-normalized ratio scores reveal anomalies that static equilibrium scoring can mask. A label-free selector chooses the score family from feature homophily, edge density, and feature dimension, then ranks candidates by fitted-null deviation and rank stability. On 11 benchmarks and without labels at any step, EB-GAD has the best or tied-best AUROC on 9: the four financial fraud networks (up to 3.7M nodes), the YelpChi and Amazon review graphs, Weibo, Reddit and Facebook, with margins of up to 21.7 points. It is second on BlogCatalog and ACM.

## 1 Introduction

Node-level graph anomaly detection (GAD) asks which nodes look abnormal relative to both their attributes and graph neighborhoods, with applications in fraud detection, cybersecurity, and scientific networks [1, 23]. This is difficult without labels because edges are not always equally trustworthy: in homophilic graphs they reveal normal communities, while in fraud, camouflage, or heterophilic settings they can hide abnormal behavior [22, 7, 19, 34, 36, 37]. A detector must therefore decide how much graph structure should define normality (graph trust), which graph-scale variations matter, and how to turn deviations into an anomaly score.

Most GAD methods make these choices indirectly through architectures, message passing, reconstruction or contrastive losses, and hyperparameter sweeps [6, 17, 10, 21, 13, 43, 16, 25]. This makes scores hard to interpret and unstable across graph regimes: in native-score evaluation, several baselines fall below chance on Elliptic and Facebook (Table 1). It also leaves no way to ask whether a node is anomalous because it is graph-rough, slow to match a normal template, or large in residual magnitude.

EB-GAD makes these choices explicit. We fit a structured prior in which normal features relax toward a graph-filtered template, while empirical Bayes (EB) selects graph trust and length-scale from the residual-field likelihood [27, 8]; since anomalies are rare, this likelihood is dominated by normal nodes. The prior is the stationary law of a graph-aware generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (GOU) process [41] with graph Matérn precision [3, 38]. This connects modeling to scoring: starting from neutral features, how much control effort is needed to reach the observed node as graph modes relax? The answer is a finite-horizon control energy, available in closed form. Varying the relaxation horizon Γ\Gamma and endpoint tolerance λc\lambda_{c} gives a bank of such scores. The bank keeps one fitted prior but tests different views: short-horizon effort, equilibrium roughness, and scale-normalized roughness.

We call the resulting framework EB-GAD (Empirical-Bayes GAD) and describe it as training-free. By this we mean that no neural parameters are learned, no gradient descent or epochs are run, and no labels or validation sets are used. EB-GAD does fit three scalars (ρ,κ,γ)(\rho,\kappa,\gamma), by maximizing a closed-form likelihood on the unlabeled graph (a clamped Newton iteration in ρ\rho, small grids over κ\kappa and γ\gamma), and it ranks a fixed bank of closed-form scores with a label-free selector whose grids and constants are fixed in advance (Appendix B.4). Training-free does not mean hyperparameter-free; a longer name is closed-form scoring with empirical-Bayes calibration. Each ingredient (graph Gaussian priors, likelihood-based fitting, OU processes, Mahalanobis scores) is established; our contribution is their calibrated composition and the evidence that it works without training:

- 1.

Anomaly score as finite-horizon control energy.
We extend equilibrium graph-Mahalanobis scoring to a finite-horizon bank using the GOU’s closed-form Gaussian transitions. The scores (CΓ,λcC_{\Gamma,\lambda_{c}}, C​RΓ,λcCR_{\Gamma,\lambda_{c}}, J⋆J^{\star}, RR) share one EB-fitted graph precision, hence one plug-in null; J⋆J^{\star} is a limiting case, not the whole model.

- 2.

Label-free selection over the bank.
A predeclared selector chooses among equilibrium, control-energy, control-ratio, and profile scores without labels. Graph statistics pick the family; within it, fitted-null deviation and neighboring-rank stability pick a stable score rather than an isolated AUROC-favored configuration.

- 3.

Training-free empirical Bayes prior selection.
The linear-Gaussian structure makes the residual-space likelihood closed-form. Algorithm 1 fits the prior by scalar optimizations; its parameters are interpretable (ρ\rho: graph trust; κ\kappa: inverse length-scale; γ\gamma: template bandwidth).

On 11 benchmarks and without labels at any step, EB-GAD has the best or tied-best AUROC on 9: the four financial fraud networks (up to 3.7M nodes), the YelpChi and Amazon review graphs, Weibo, Reddit and Facebook, with margins of up to 21.7 points. It is second on BlogCatalog and ACM. Section 4 and Section 5 state where it trails, and which entries depend on a design choice made for this version.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Graph Anomaly Detection

Graph anomaly detection (GAD) identifies atypical nodes whose attributes and structural interactions deviate from graph regularities. We focus on node-level GAD on attributed graphs [1, 23]. Formally, given 𝒢=(𝒱,ℰ,X)\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},X) with |𝒱|=n|\mathcal{V}|=n, adjacency AA, and node features X∈ℝn×DX\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times D}, unsupervised node-level GAD outputs scores si=f⁡(i,A,X)s_{i}=f(i,A,X) whose large values rank nodes i∈𝒱i\in\mathcal{V} as anomalous. Existing methods span reconstruction/generative approaches [6, 17, 10, 16], self-supervised/contrastive methods [21, 13, 43], and one-class methods [25]. In fraud settings, they must also handle class imbalance, camouflage, and heterophily [22, 7, 19, 34, 36, 37]. Across these families, graph trust, graph-spectral weighting, and anomaly scoring are often tied to architectures and hyperparameter sweeps.
EB-GAD instead casts these decisions as parameters of a single linear-Gaussian model and selects them from data without labels.

### 2.2 Empirical Bayes for Anomaly Detection

Unsupervised anomaly detection can be cast as evaluating Shannon surprise under a normal-data model, using s⁡(xi)=−log⁡p⁡(xi∣φ)s(x_{i})=-\log p(x_{i}\mid\varphi). We use φ\varphi for prior or marginal-model hyperparameters, not arbitrary trained weights; in EB-GAD these are graph trust, graph length-scale, and template bandwidth. Many methods approximate this score through density estimation [4], reconstruction error [6, 10], or representation-space distance [25, 21]; in each case, the fitted normality notion determines the anomaly score.
In empirical Bayes [27, 8], these hyperparameters are selected from data 𝒟\mathcal{D} by maximizing the marginal likelihood:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | φ⋆=arg⁡maxφ⁡log⁡p⁡(𝒟∣φ),\varphi^{\star}=\arg\max_{\varphi}\;\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\varphi), | | (1) |

which integrates out latent variables when present and balances fit against complexity (Occam’s razor) [8].
Since anomalies are rare, this criterion is dominated by normal nodes and φ⋆\varphi^{\star} approximately recovers a normal-data prior. When the model is conjugate (Gaussian prior with linear-Gaussian likelihood), the marginal likelihood is available in closed form; otherwise one typically optimizes a variational lower bound (ELBO). In our setting, empirical Bayes is applied to the residual field induced by a low-pass graph-filtered template rather than directly to the raw features. The likelihood is a graph-coupled density over the full residual field, while the node score is the nodewise contribution to the same joint Gaussian energy. Bayesian outlier detection has been explored in non-graph settings [28, 32]; in graph anomaly detection, prior structure is usually fixed rather than selected from data.

### 2.3 Generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Dynamics

We need a normal-data model that can encode prior knowledge about where node features should concentrate, while remaining tractable enough for label-free fitting. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process provides exactly this: a linear Gaussian model whose stationary mean can be set to a template 𝐦\mathbf{m}. We use its matrix-drift generalization,

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | d​Xt=−α⁡(t)​Q¯ρ​(Xt−𝐦)​d​t+2​α​(t)​d​Wt,dX_{t}=-\alpha(t)\,\bar{Q}_{\rho}\,(X_{t}-\mathbf{m})\,dt+\sqrt{2\alpha(t)}\,dW_{t}, | | (2) |

where α⁡(t)>0\alpha(t)>0 is a scalar schedule, WtW_{t} is Brownian motion, and Q¯ρ\bar{Q}_{\rho} is a symmetric positive definite (SPD) drift operator. Coupling the noise scale to α⁡(t)\alpha(t) gives stationary distribution 𝒩⁡(𝐦,Q¯ρ−1)\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m},\bar{Q}_{\rho}^{-1}) and closed-form Gaussian transitions. Section 3.1 instantiates 𝐦\mathbf{m} as a graph-filtered template and Q¯ρ\bar{Q}_{\rho} as a graph-aware precision, injecting graph prior knowledge into both the stationary mean and covariance while keeping EB fitting and node scoring closed form.

## 3 Graph Anomaly Detection via Finite-Horizon Control

The components of EB-GAD are standard: a graph Gaussian prior, the OU dynamics attached to it, and Gaussian quadratic scores. The contribution is their composition, calibrated by EB and selected without labels. We therefore state the model first and its control reading afterwards: §3.1–3.3 define a graph-aware GOU and derive its bank of finite-horizon scores; §3.4 fits the prior and selects a score.

### 3.1 Graph-Aware GOU Process

#### Notation.

We use three forms of the same node-feature data: input matrix X∈ℝn×DX\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times D}, its row-major vectorization x¯=vec⁡(X)∈ℝn​D\bar{x}=\operatorname{vec}(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{nD}, and the GOU state Xt∈ℝn​DX_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{nD} (with X0=0X_{0}=0 and XT=x¯X_{T}=\bar{x} at scoring time; lowercase xTx_{T} in SOC formulae denotes the same realization). The objects X,M,Δ,ΔΓX,M,\Delta,\Delta_{\Gamma} are used in either n×Dn\times D matrix or ℝn​D\mathbb{R}^{nD} vec form depending on context (covariance equations use the vec form; nodewise scoring uses the matrix); [A]i,:∈ℝD[A]_{i,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} denotes the ii-th row of an n×Dn\times D matrix.

#### Reference dynamics.

Let Wt∈ℝn​DW_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{nD} be a standard Brownian motion. We define a graph-aware GOU reference process around a template 𝐦∈ℝn​D\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{nD}:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | d​Xt=−α⁡(t)​Q¯ρ​(Xt−𝐦)​d​t+2​α​(t)​d​Wt,t∈[0,T],dX_{t}=-\alpha(t)\,\bar{Q}_{\rho}\,\bigl(X_{t}-\mathbf{m}\bigr)\,dt+\sqrt{2\alpha(t)}\,dW_{t},\qquad t\in[0,T], | | (3) |

where α⁡(t)>0\alpha(t)>0 is a deterministic scalar schedule and Q¯ρ\bar{Q}_{\rho} is a graph-aware precision operator.
The matrix drift Q¯ρ\bar{Q}_{\rho} embeds the graph into the dynamics: in eigencoordinates of Q¯ρ\bar{Q}_{\rho}, mode jj relaxes at rate α⁡(t)​qj\alpha(t)q_{j}, so smooth (low Laplacian frequency, small qjq_{j}) modes drift slowly toward the template while rough modes equilibrate fast.
The closed-form Gaussian transition that follows therefore inherits a graph-spectral schedule of relaxation times.

#### Role of the dynamics for static data.

We do not claim that the data evolved by OU dynamics, and static data could not support such a claim. The choice is representational. Among linear diffusions with isotropic noise, (3) is the unique one, up to time scale, that is reversible with stationary law 𝒩⁡(𝐦,Q¯ρ−1)\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m},\bar{Q}_{\rho}^{-1}); it is the canonical dynamics of the graph Gaussian prior that EB fits and adds no modeling freedom at equilibrium. What it adds is a horizon-indexed family of closed-form quadratic scores that share this one prior, so that every horizon is calibrated by the same fitted law; a heat or polynomial filter bank changes the implied prior at each scale. The Brownian increments are independent of the current state by definition of the linear-Gaussian transition, not as an assumption on the data: a state-dependent innovation would be absorbed into a different drift and stationary precision, which is what (ρ,κ)(\rho,\kappa) fit, and since only the stationary law and its finite-horizon interpolations enter the scores, such dependence is not identifiable from a static graph.

#### Precision blend: graph trust and inverse length-scale.

We parameterize the precision operator with a graph trust parameter ρ∈[0,1]\rho\in[0,1] and Matérn inverse length-scale κ≥0\kappa\geq 0:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | Q¯ρ:=Qρ⊗ID,Qρ:=ρ​Qprior+(1−ρ)​In,Qprior=(κ2​In+L)ν,\bar{Q}_{\rho}:=Q_{\rho}\otimes I_{D},\qquad Q_{\rho}:=\rho\,Q_{\mathrm{prior}}+(1-\rho)\,I_{n},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{prior}}=(\kappa^{2}I_{n}+L)^{\nu}, | | (4) |

where ⊗\otimes denotes the Kronecker product, ν>0\nu>0 controls the spectral roll-off, i.e., how quickly precision grows with graph frequency (fixed to ν=1\nu=1 throughout), and LL is the Laplacian of the selected graph operator (the input graph by default, or a predeclared feature-affinity reweighting that keeps the same edge set; Appendix B.4). In eigenspace, the precision on mode jj is qj=ρ​(κ2+λj)ν+(1−ρ)q_{j}=\rho(\kappa^{2}+\lambda_{j})^{\nu}+(1-\rho), where λj\lambda_{j} is the jj-th selected-operator eigenvalue. The two parameters play distinct roles:
ρ\rho controls the overall graph trust: ρ=1\rho=1 gives full graph-spectral precision, while ρ=0\rho=0 gives isotropic precision. κ\kappa is the Matérn inverse length-scale: modes with λj≫κ2\lambda_{j}\gg\kappa^{2} are dominated by graph-frequency weighting, while modes with λj≪κ2\lambda_{j}\ll\kappa^{2} share the approximately constant precision level (1−ρ)+ρ​κ2​ν(1-\rho)+\rho\kappa^{2\nu}. Thus κ\kappa controls where the transition from low-frequency floor to graph-spectral growth occurs, rather than forcing the low-frequency precision to equal the isotropic value one. This follows from Matérn kernels on graphs [3, 38].

#### Prior template.

The template 𝐦∈ℝn​D\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{nD} encodes a prior notion of normal node attributes, defined as a low-pass graph-filtered version of the features:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | 𝐦:=S¯γ,ν​x¯,Sγ,ν:=(γ2​In+L)−ν,γ>0,ν>0,\mathbf{m}:=\bar{S}_{\gamma,\nu}\,\bar{x},\qquad S_{\gamma,\nu}:=(\gamma^{2}I_{n}+L)^{-\nu},\qquad\gamma>0,\ \nu>0, | | (5) |

where S¯γ,ν:=Sγ,ν⊗ID\bar{S}_{\gamma,\nu}:=S_{\gamma,\nu}\otimes I_{D} is the unnormalized Matérn resolvent used in our implementation [3, 38].
It is low-pass in graph frequency; because it is unnormalized, the profiled γ\gamma controls both bandwidth and low-frequency scale, and eigenvalues 1/(γ2+λj)ν1/(\gamma^{2}+\lambda_{j})^{\nu} can exceed one when γ2+λj<1\gamma^{2}+\lambda_{j}<1 (e.g., DC gain γ−2​ν\gamma^{-2\nu} on a Laplacian zero mode). We treat this as a fixed template family rather than a post-hoc normalization: EB fitting and scoring always use the same chosen residual operator, while a unit-DC-gain variant would simply define a different predeclared template family.
The low-pass construction is canonical, while heterophilic high-dimensional regimes may use the same Matérn construction with a predeclared affinity-weighted template without changing the Gaussian or control-energy derivation.
Define Tγ,ν:=In−Sγ,νT_{\gamma,\nu}:=I_{n}-S_{\gamma,\nu} and Δ=Tγ,ν​X\Delta=T_{\gamma,\nu}X.
All likelihoods and scores are computed on this residual field rather than on raw features; truncated eigenspace and null-mode details are given in Appendix A.1.

### 3.2 Finite-Horizon Generative Model and Control Energy

We score a node by the dynamical effort needed to generate its observed features from a feature-neutral start under the graph-aware GOU.
Concretely: imagine steering the GOU from the origin to the observed endpoint x¯\bar{x} at time Γ\Gamma; the minimum steering energy (under hard endpoint matching) is exactly the negative log-density of the GOU’s unconditioned endpoint distribution, up to a constant.
This identification turns the closed-form Gaussian transition kernel into an anomaly score, the finite-horizon control energy, and it lets a single fitted GOU yield a bank of scores by varying the horizon Γ\Gamma and the endpoint tolerance λc\lambda_{c}. It is the minimum control energy of a GOU bridge to one observed endpoint: no coupling or transport plan is optimized, so it is not an optimal-transport cost.

#### Control energy interpretation.

For a fixed template 𝐦\mathbf{m}, the conditional Gaussian score induced by (6) equals, up to an xTx_{T}-independent constant, the minimum control energy in a stochastic optimal control (SOC) formulation, since both reduce to the quadratic form 12​(xT−μΓ)⊤​ΣΓ−1​(xT−μΓ)\frac{1}{2}(x_{T}-\mu_{\Gamma})^{\top}\Sigma_{\Gamma}^{-1}(x_{T}-\mu_{\Gamma}).

### 3.3 Per-Node Anomaly Scoring

We now decompose the global Gaussian energy of (6) into per-node contributions, then generalize from hard endpoint matching to a finite-horizon bank parameterized by an endpoint tolerance.

#### Graph signal model.

The Kronecker structure Q¯ρ=Qρ⊗ID\bar{Q}_{\rho}=Q_{\rho}\otimes I_{D} means the DD feature dimensions are conditionally independent given the graph.
At scoring time the GOU endpoint equals the observation, XT=x¯X_{T}=\bar{x}, so the realized finite-horizon residual is the matrix ΔΓ∈ℝn×D\Delta_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times D} obtained by reshaping x¯−μΓ\bar{x}-\mu_{\Gamma}, with rows δΓ,i:=[ΔΓ]i,:=[X]i,:−[μΓ]i,:\delta_{\Gamma,i}:=[\Delta_{\Gamma}]_{i,:}=[X]_{i,:}-[\mu_{\Gamma}]_{i,:}, representing DD conditionally independent graph signals.
Its hard-endpoint Gaussian log-likelihood, up to the additive constant −k​D2​log⁡2​π-\tfrac{kD}{2}\log 2\pi, is

Equation (11) is the hard-endpoint case (λc→∞\lambda_{c}\to\infty) of a more general soft-endpoint Gaussian.
We retain the finite-horizon GOU transition instead of taking Γ→∞\Gamma\to\infty, because short horizons ask whether the node is surprising before the template has fully relaxed, while long horizons approach the equilibrium residual Δ=x¯−𝐦\Delta=\bar{x}-\mathbf{m}.

#### Finite-horizon score bank.

Hard endpoint matching is a knife-edge: it asserts that the GOU sample at time Γ\Gamma exactly equals the observation x¯\bar{x}. Allowing endpoint uncertainty λc<∞\lambda_{c}<\infty tests whether the anomaly evidence persists under softer matching, where small deviations from the noiseless endpoint are tolerated.
We model this by adding observation noise: if ZΓ∼𝒩⁡(μΓ,ΣΓ)Z_{\Gamma}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\Gamma},\Sigma_{\Gamma}) and x¯=ZΓ+η\bar{x}=Z_{\Gamma}+\eta with η∼𝒩⁡(0,λc−1​I)\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda_{c}^{-1}I), then marginalizing ZΓZ_{\Gamma} gives x¯∼𝒩⁡(μΓ,ΣΓ+λc−1​I)\bar{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\Gamma},\Sigma_{\Gamma}+\lambda_{c}^{-1}I).

Together, the family of CΓ,λcC_{\Gamma,\lambda_{c}} and C​RΓ,λcCR_{\Gamma,\lambda_{c}} at varying (Γ,λc)(\Gamma,\lambda_{c}), with their Γ,λc→∞\Gamma,\lambda_{c}\to\infty limits J⋆J^{\star} and RR, constitutes the finite-horizon score bank (the bank).

### 3.4 Empirical Bayes Prior Selection

For each candidate template bandwidth γ\gamma, empirical Bayes selects (ρ,κ)(\rho,\kappa) from the exact equilibrium residual-space likelihood, and the fitted precision is reused by every finite-horizon score in (14)–(15). EB estimates the graph-Gaussian geometry, while the finite-horizon bank probes how anomaly evidence appears along the GOU relaxation path. Because Δ=Tγ,ν​X\Delta=T_{\gamma,\nu}X is γ\gamma-dependent, the outer γ\gamma step is residual-space profile selection: it optimizes the Gaussian fit in the transformed residual field and omits the raw-feature Jacobian D​∑j=1klog⁡|1−(γ2+λj)−ν|D\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log|1-(\gamma^{2}+\lambda_{j})^{-\nu}|. The selected γ⋆\gamma^{\star} is used by the finite-horizon families, whose pp-values are computed under the fitted law of that residual; it is also the bandwidth that removes the most feature energy (Table 14). The equilibrium anchor instead uses the grid value γa\gamma_{a} nearest to the graph-statistic center γ0\gamma_{0}, with its own EB fit (Appendix C.1).

#### Four score families.

The bank is used through four families:

- •

Equilibrium anchor (J⋆J^{\star} or RR): the stationary graph-Mahalanobis energy or its scale-normalized ratio. It is the default, used when the finite-horizon diagnostics are not stable enough to justify leaving the stationary score, and on sparse, heavily truncated graphs.

- •

Control energy (CΓ,λcC_{\Gamma,\lambda_{c}}): the absolute effort to reach a node at a finite horizon. It is used when residual magnitude is itself informative: on dense graphs with homophilic low-dimensional features, or with high-dimensional features of near-zero homophily (then on the affinity template).

- •

Control ratio (C​RΓ,λcCR_{\Gamma,\lambda_{c}}): the share of a node’s residual that the fitted graph geometry makes expensive. It is used when residual magnitude would otherwise dominate: sparse graphs with high-dimensional, weakly homophilic features.

- •

Profile aggregation: per-node pp-values of the bank members under the shared plug-in null, combined across horizons and tolerances. It is used when evidence is spread over horizons: very dense graphs, or graphs whose profile diagnostics pass.

#### Label-free selector.

After the EB fit, a fixed rule chooses one family using only label-free statistics: cosine feature homophily hh, edge density ded_{e}, feature dimension DxD_{x}, and the predeclared truncation kk.
Within the chosen family, scores in the admissible pool ℬ\mathcal{B} are ranked by percentile-normalized quality plus neighboring-rank stability,
A⁡(r)=pctℬ⁡(Q⁡(r))+pctℬ⁡(S⁡(r))A(r)=\operatorname{pct}_{\mathcal{B}}(Q(r))+\operatorname{pct}_{\mathcal{B}}(S(r)), where Q⁡(r)Q(r) is a family-specific fitted-null diagnostic and S⁡(r)=medianr′∈N⁡(r)⁡corr⁡(rank⁡(sr),rank⁡(sr′))S(r)=\operatorname{median}_{r^{\prime}\in N(r)}\operatorname{corr}(\operatorname{rank}(s_{r}),\operatorname{rank}(s_{r^{\prime}})) is local rank-stability over predeclared neighbors N⁡(r)N(r) of the node-score vector sr∈ℝns_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}; ranks average ties, so the selection does not depend on the order of the nodes.
The selector is label-free model selection: the graph statistics decide which family’s null model fits the regime, and the within-family diagnostics are goodness-of-fit statistics against that family’s plug-in null. It is sound in this sense, but it is not derived from a single principle: its cutoffs are implementation defaults, identical on all datasets, whose provenance and tested sensitivity are given in Appendix B.4 with the ordered rules.

The likelihood is concave in ρ\rho, while κ\kappa and γ\gamma are searched on bounded grids (Remark 1); Remark 2 discusses the sensitivity of the fit to anomalies.

## 5 Scope, Limitations and Conclusion

EB-GAD is a training-free empirical-Bayes model for node-level graph anomaly detection: a graph-aware GOU defines a bank of finite-horizon control-energy scores, the residual-space likelihood selects the graph precision without labels, and a fixed selector chooses one of four score families. The equilibrium Mahalanobis score is one limiting case; aggregating or normalizing the finite-horizon family under its shared null adds 3.63.6 to 4.74.7 AUROC points on three datasets and is at parity elsewhere. Run without labels, EB-GAD has the best or tied-best AUROC on 9 of 11 benchmarks.

Scope and limitations.
(i) Selection. The model does not identify the template bandwidth: the likelihood sets it for the finite-horizon families and graph statistics set it for the anchor, a rule adopted after the first had been evaluated and without which Weibo and Reddit are 92.592.5 and 48.548.5. The selector is label-free and stable under perturbation of its cutoffs, but it is not derived from one principle: its cutoffs are implementation defaults developed on the benchmarks of this paper, and we do not claim that they transfer to other graph families.
(ii) Gaussian model. In a controlled test heavy-tailed residuals cost at most 0.70.7 points and a two-component normal population 99 to 1919; binarized features fall outside the Gaussian residual model (AUROC below 6161) and need a categorical likelihood.
(iii) Contamination. With up to 24%24\% contamination the fitted prior moves little and detection of the original anomalies loses 1.11.1 points on Weibo; large contiguous anomalous regions, which can become their own context, were not studied.
(iv) Graph operators. BlogCatalog and ACM trail TAM, and high-pass or band-pass templates do not close the gap; heterophily is handled only through feature-affinity reweighting.
(v) Truncated spectra. With many small components the truncated eigenbasis is not unique; on YelpChi the AUROC ranges over two points across node orders.
(vi) Fairness. Scores on user graphs can encode degree and activity biases and should be audited across user segments before deployment.

### B.4 Selector and Hyperparameter Specification

This section states the rule exactly as it is run; the released code contains the same implementation and the script that regenerates Table 1.
No rule takes a label as input.
The cutoffs are implementation defaults, identical on all 11 datasets, not derived quantities: they were fixed during the development of the method on the benchmark families studied here, so they are part of its engineering. #### Preprocessing, spectrum and grids.

Features are z-scored. For the equilibrium and profile families, features with Dx>100D_{x}>100 are projected on their first 64 principal components; PCA is a deterministic unsupervised linear map, so the closed-form results apply in the projected space.
The full Laplacian spectrum is used when n<20,000n<20{,}000; larger graphs use the kk smallest eigenpairs with k=500k=500 (n<105n<10^{5}), 300300 (n<5⋅105n<5{\cdot}10^{5}), 200200 (n<106n<10^{6}) or 128128. ### B.6 Resource Utility and Scalability Measures

Baselines were trained and evaluated with their reported hyperparameters on NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs. The EB-GAD results of Table 1 were regenerated on CPU. For medium and large graphs we use a truncated eigendecomposition; the retained kk for each dataset is reported in Table 5.

To scale EB-GAD to large graphs, we adopt the following efficiency measures:

- •

Truncated eigendecomposition: For graphs with n≥20n\geq 20K nodes we use the Locally Optimal Block Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (LOBPCG [14]) method, with a seeded initial block, to compute the kk smallest eigenpairs of the sparse Laplacian; the basis is then orthonormalized and Ritz-refined in double precision. These are the smooth graph modes used by the low-pass template and the Matérn precision; unretained rough modes are omitted from the likelihood and the control-energy numerator, while ratio scores still normalize by the full nodewise residual norm. The eigenvector matrix is (n×k)(n\times k) rather than (n×n)(n\times n), reducing memory from O⁡(n2)O(n^{2}) to O⁡(n​k)O(nk); kk is scaled by graph size (Table 5).

- •

Eigenspace operations: All graph filters and prior operations use products V⁡(𝐟⁡(λ)⊙(V⊤​𝐱))V(\mathbf{f}(\lambda)\odot(V^{\top}\mathbf{x})) in O⁡(n⋅k⋅D)O(n\cdot k\cdot D) per step, avoiding dense matrix exponentials.

- •

Sparse Laplacian: For graphs with n≥8n\geq 8K nodes, we use a sparse construction to avoid materializing dense n×nn\times n matrices.

- •

Eigen cache: The dense precision matrix QQ is skipped for large graphs; all scoring operations use the precomputed eigen cache instead.

### B.8 Dimensionality Reduction and Nonlinear Extensions

The main text uses PCA as a training-free dimensionality reduction that preserves the linear-Gaussian structure.
Here we discuss the theoretical landscape of dimensionality reduction for EB-GAD and report results with trained nonlinear encoders.

#### Linear reductions preserve the Gaussian structure.

Feature-space linear maps preserve Gaussianity. If z=(In⊗W)​xz=(I_{n}\otimes W)x and x∼𝒩⁡(𝐦,Qρ−1⊗ID)x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m},Q_{\rho}^{-1}\otimes I_{D}), then
z∼𝒩⁡((In⊗W)​𝐦,Qρ−1⊗W​W⊤)z\sim\mathcal{N}((I_{n}\otimes W)\mathbf{m},Q_{\rho}^{-1}\otimes WW^{\top}).
For PCA with orthonormal retained components (and after standardization), W​W⊤=IpWW^{\top}=I_{p}, so the same closed-form anomaly scores and residual-space likelihood apply exactly in the projected space.
PCA is deterministic, unsupervised, and maximizes retained variance among linear projections.

#### Nonlinear reductions break the Gaussian structure.

When a nonlinear encoder fθ:ℝD→ℝpf_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{p} is applied, the latent features z=fθ​(x)z=f_{\theta}(x) are no longer Gaussian even if xx is.
The residual-space likelihood objective is no longer available in closed form, and a variational bound is needed for prior selection.

#### Variational formulation (ELBO).

For this nonlinear-encoder extension only, one could construct a terminally conditioned GOU bridge via Doob’s hh-transform on a discrete time grid and optimize a reconstruction-plus-KL variational objective in the spirit of learned diffusion-bridge/reverse-transition models [44, 41, 45]. The bound would combine a per-step KL between the analytic bridge transition qφq_{\varphi} and the learned reverse transition pθp_{\theta} with an endpoint reconstruction term; in the linear-Gaussian case (linear encoder or no encoder), the bridge transitions match the true posterior exactly, the variational gap vanishes, and the bound reduces to the exact residual-space objective (17).
This bridge is not used in the main EB-GAD pipeline, which relies on the unconditioned GOU transition law; we mention the ELBO only to indicate how a future nonlinear-encoder extension would inherit the empirical-Bayes structure.

#### Non-neural nonlinear options.

Beyond neural encoders, several non-neural nonlinear methods could extend the framework:

- •

Kernel PCA: maps features to a reproducing kernel Hilbert space via a kernel function (e.g., RBF). Non-parametric and training-free, but the infinite-dimensional feature space requires truncation, and the Gaussian assumption is approximate.

- •

Random Fourier Features [26]: approximates kernel PCA with explicit features z⁡(x)=cos⁡(W​x+b)z(x)=\cos(Wx+b) for random WW. Deterministic, finite-dimensional, and training-free, but the nonlinear map makes the likelihood objective approximate.

- •

GPLVM [15]: a principled Bayesian nonlinear latent variable model with a natural variational formulation. Requires optimization of variational parameters, making it no longer training-free.

These represent a spectrum from fully closed-form (PCA) to fully variational (GPLVM), trading tractability for expressive power.
We leave exploration of these extensions to future work.

Where it hurts, two causes are plausible: a nonlinear encoder breaks the Gaussian structure, so the exact residual-space objective (17) no longer applies to the latent features, and it can distort the relation between graph frequencies and feature variation on which the scores operate.
The training-free pipeline is also deterministic given the eigenbasis, whereas trained encoders add seed-dependent variance.

#### Protocol.

The procedure of Appendix B.4 follows Algorithm 1 and the modeling details of Appendix A.1 as submitted, and no label enters any step before evaluation. Four points are made explicit. #### Bandwidth of the anchor.

The submitted Algorithm 1 selects one bandwidth, by the residual-space likelihood, for every score. In the first regeneration for this version the anchor used that bandwidth, which gave 92.592.5 on Weibo and 48.548.5 on Reddit. The residual Δ=Tγ,ν​X\Delta=T_{\gamma,\nu}X is a different function of the data at each bandwidth, and its likelihood is largest at the grid value whose residual map removes the largest share of the feature energy, on all eleven datasets (7979 to 95%95\% on the six full-spectrum datasets; Table 14). The criterion therefore measures how much of the features the template explains. The pp-values of the finite-horizon families are computed under the fitted law of that residual and keep γ⋆\gamma^{\star}; how well a single energy of the residual ranks anomalies is a different question, which the likelihood does not address.

In this version the anchor uses γa\gamma_{a}, the grid value nearest to γ0\gamma_{0}. The center γ0=(0.5+h)/max⁡(de,10)/10\gamma_{0}=(0.5+h)/\sqrt{\max(d_{e},10)/10} is small for dense graphs with low feature homophily and large for sparse homophilic ones. For γ<1\gamma<1 the residual filter 1−(γ2+λ)−11-(\gamma^{2}+\lambda)^{-1} is negative and large in magnitude on low graph frequencies, so the anchor reads the smoothed signal of a node’s neighborhood (clustered anomalies, as on Weibo); for γ≥1\gamma\geq 1 it is close to the deviation of the node itself (Reddit and the fraud graphs). The center and the grid are those of the submitted code, the rule is the same on every dataset, and no label enters it.

The decision to adopt the rule, however, was taken after the anchors at both bandwidths had been evaluated on the labels, and its effect is not uniform. Against the equilibrium score at γ⋆\gamma^{\star}, the one at γa\gamma_{a} is higher on Weibo, Reddit and Amazon, lower on BlogCatalog, Facebook and Elliptic++, and identical on the five datasets where the two bandwidths coincide (Table 14). Of the six datasets where they differ, three are routed to an anchor: Table 1 gains 2.62.6 points on Weibo and 12.112.1 on Reddit and loses 1.21.2 on Elliptic++. The rule is therefore not a blind choice, and Table 13 reports the alternatives next to it: the anchor at the likelihood-selected bandwidth, and the anchor averaged over the three bandwidths of the grid (mean percentile rank), which gives 95.395.3 on Weibo and 60.760.7 on Reddit, raises Elliptic and Elliptic++ (to 75.175.1 and 75.975.9) and lowers YelpChi and DGraph (to 64.064.0 and 66.166.1). Using γ0\gamma_{0} itself instead of the nearest grid value gives 95.595.5 on Weibo and 59.859.8 on Reddit and lowers YelpChi to 62.962.9 and Elliptic to 70.870.8. The rule is restricted to the anchor: applied to every family it would set γ=0.5\gamma=0.5 on Facebook, where the scale-entropy diagnostic then fails and the rules fall back to J⋆J^{\star} (47.447.4 instead of 91.491.4). The choice of the score type by NullKS is the same in every node order at every anchor bandwidth; among the other grid values it changes with the node order on Reddit at γ=1\gamma=1 (NullKS of RR: 0.450.45 and 0.240.24) and on YelpChi at γ=2\gamma=2. For the anchor the bandwidth is thus a declared part of the prior and not a fitted quantity, and the Weibo and Reddit entries should be read with this in mind.

### C.3 Isolation of the Spectral Precision Family

A reviewer asked whether the gains come from the OU formulation or would be obtained by any spectral scoring of the same residuals. For each dataset we freeze the operator, the template, PCA, the truncation, the residuals and the label-free selection, and change only the spectral precision: the EB-fitted GOU precision with its finite-horizon members, the GOU precision at fixed (ρ,κ)=(0.5,1)(\rho,\kappa)=(0.5,1), heat-kernel precisions, polynomial precisions, and the identity (Table 16). One configuration per dataset is shared by the five families, so the columns are compared with each other and not with Table 1, which selects over families and pools.

On Elliptic, Elliptic++ and T-Finance the GOU bank leads the best generic family by 12.612.6, 11.611.6 and 10.210.2 points under identical label-free selection, and on T-Finance the EB fit alone adds 23.623.6 points over the fixed GOU precision. The mechanism is selectability rather than the quality of the best score: on Elliptic and Elliptic++ the best members of all five families are within 1.21.2 points of each other (74.474.4 to 75.275.2, 73.773.7 to 74.974.9), so the generic banks contain an equally good score, but their label-free selection falls to 5858 to 6262. Elsewhere no family is consistently better. The GOU bank is within 1.11.1 points of the best generic selection on Weibo, YelpChi, BlogCatalog, ACM and DGraph; a polynomial bank is ahead on Reddit (+3.3+3.3, all families near chance) and Amazon (+6.8+6.8), and a heat-kernel bank is ahead on Facebook by 11.611.6 points and reaches 91.691.6, the level of Table 1. We therefore claim no superiority of the OU structure on those datasets; the claim is specific to the three financial graphs.

### C.4 Horizon Axis: Matched Single-Score Comparison

A reviewer asked for a direct comparison of equilibrium-only scoring with finite-horizon scoring, with the fitted model and all other components fixed. Two comparisons answer it. Table 2 in the main text compares the score that the selector uses with the equilibrium score of the same configuration. Table 17 is narrower: one score type on both sides, a single finite horizon, no endpoint-tolerance grid, no fusion and no aggregation. DGraph is not included (its spectrum is computed on the labeled subgraph by a separate script).

At the granularity of single scores the answer is parity. The best finite-horizon member by AUROC is within 0.30.3 points of its equilibrium limit on nine of ten datasets and 1.41.4 below it on T-Finance; when NullKS picks the horizon, the finite score is within 0.20.2 points of the limit on eight datasets and below it on ACM (−1.6-1.6) and T-Finance (−1.4-1.4). No single finite-horizon score reproduces the gains of Table 2 (+3.6+3.6 on Facebook, +4.7+4.7 on ACM and T-Finance). Those gains come from what is done with the family: aggregation of pp-values across horizons (Facebook, T-Finance) and scale normalization with stability-ranked fusion (ACM). The finite-horizon contribution is therefore not one fortunate horizon. It is that the fitted GOU attaches one prior, hence one plug-in null, to every horizon, which is what makes the family comparable and aggregatable without labels; the equilibrium limit is a single member and cannot be aggregated.

#### Cutoff sensitivity.

The ordered rules of Appendix B.4 contain 21 cutoffs. We multiply each one by 0.750.75 and 1.251.25, and by 0.50.5 and 1.51.5, one at a time, and re-evaluate the rules on the 11 datasets with the diagnostics of the runs of Table 1. At ±25%\pm 25\% the selected family and branch are unchanged in 459 of 462 evaluations (99.4%99.4\%), at ±50%\pm 50\% in 450 of 462 (97.4%97.4\%). Scaling all cutoffs jointly by 0.750.75 changes the selection on 2 of 11 datasets (Reddit and Facebook); scaling them by 1.251.25 changes it on one (Weibo). Table 18 lists the fifteen changes. Every one of them lowers the AUROC, by 2.22.2 to 44.044.0 points. Eleven select an equilibrium anchor instead of the score of Table 1, and the cost is largest where NullKS then selects J⋆J^{\star} at the anchor bandwidth: on Facebook (six changes, 47.447.4 instead of 91.491.4), whose routing to profile aggregation depends on four graph-statistic cutoffs and on the upper end of the mixture-mass window (π=0.81\pi=0.81), and on Elliptic++ (two changes, 30.330.3 instead of 72.572.5), where rule 1 otherwise fixes RR. Two reroute Weibo from the anchor to profile aggregation (−2.6-2.6) and two reroute Reddit from the anchor to the control-energy family (−15.7-15.7). The selections are therefore stable under sizable perturbations of single cutoffs, but where a selection changes the cost can be large, and the choice of the score type by NullKS is the fragile step.

#### Misspecification.

Table 22 is a controlled stress test of the Gaussian residual model on stochastic block model graphs, with the same pipeline in every column and seed-matched conditions. ### C.7 Graph Structure, Edge Uncertainty and Neighboring Anomalies

#### A worked example.

A reviewer asked how the network structure enters the score and how uncertainty in the edges is reflected. We use Zachary’s karate club with the connectivity profiles (rows of the adjacency matrix) as features and run the pipeline of the paper (EB fit, equilibrium score J⋆J^{\star}). The three most anomalous profiles are those of the two faction leaders (individuals 34 and 1) and of individual 33. Zachary’s published matrix is inconsistent about the edge between individuals 23 and 34; we score the graph with and without it. The three largest changes of the score are at individuals 33, 34 and 23, and 98.8%98.8\% of the total absolute change falls on the two endpoints and their neighbors (18 of the 34 nodes). The ranking as a whole is stable (rank correlation 0.9890.989); eight nodes outside that neighborhood change rank, by at most five positions. Structure enters the score through two channels: the smoothed template, which the edge changes directly, and the coupling in the precision, whose weight ρ\rho is what EB fits. Because these features are the connectivity profiles themselves, EB assigns the graph’s information to the template (ρ=0.008\rho=0.008). These numbers are for γ⋆=1\gamma^{\star}=1, the likelihood-selected bandwidth, at which the template has unit gain on the constant mode. At the bandwidth nearest to γ0\gamma_{0} (0.70.7) the template amplifies low frequencies and the change is less local: 74.7%74.7\% of it falls on the same 18 nodes, and the rank correlation is 0.9270.927.

#### Neighboring anomalies.

A reviewer asked whether connected anomalies can be detected, since each could make its neighbor look normal. On Reddit we inject 5%5\% feature-swap anomalies either scattered or as connected groups of at most 10 neighbors, refit EB on each graph, and select the score without labels (Table 23). Connected groups are easier to detect, 92.092.0 to 93.693.6 against 79.979.9 to 83.083.0: a connected anomalous group shifts the smoothed template away from all of its members at once. A pairwise readout also works (Table 24): scoring an edge by the smaller of its two endpoint energies separates edges between two injected nodes from edges between two normal nodes at 94.494.4 to 96.796.7; the standardized cross-term of the quadratic form alone gives 77.577.5 to 84.084.0. We did not study large contiguous anomalous regions, which can become their own context. These results are for the EB-fitted configuration (γ⋆=0.7\gamma^{\star}=0.7), whose residual is contextual: most of the feature energy is removed and what remains is the deviation of a node from its smoothed neighborhood. At the anchor bandwidth of Reddit (γa=2\gamma_{a}=2) the residual is close to the features themselves, and a feature swap between two nodes is harder to see: the label-free choice gives 55.055.0 to 57.457.4 on connected groups (best member of the bank: 89.789.7 to 91.491.4) and 52.152.1 to 77.677.6 on scattered swaps, the smaller endpoint energy separates edges at 87.587.5 to 91.191.1, and the standardized cross-term is inverted (8.48.4 to 9.69.6). The two bandwidths therefore see different anomalies: the labeled anomalies of Reddit are found at γa\gamma_{a} and not at γ⋆\gamma^{\star}, injected swaps at γ⋆\gamma^{\star} and not at γa\gamma_{a}.

### C.8 Template Variants on BlogCatalog

A reviewer asked whether the low-pass filter limits the method to homophilic graphs and explains the BlogCatalog result. BlogCatalog has the lowest feature homophily of the benchmarks (h=0.009h=0.009) and is routed to the feature-affinity template, which reweights features by node reliability before smoothing. Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

- •

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

- •

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

- •

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
